\title{The Antagonist Reviews the Prep:\\

\begin{abstract}
A game master (GM) running a long tabletop campaign writes plans for characters the
players have not yet met again. Those plans are hard to check: the GM knows everything,
and the character knows only some of it. We ask whether a language-model agent, given
exactly what one non-player character (NPC) could know at a point in the campaign, can
act as a \emph{critic} of the GM's plans for that character.

We report a case study on one antagonist in a 59-session campaign: a drow poisoner who
was on screen at one dungeon, escaped, and has since acted only at a distance. She is
secretly using her employer's masterwork, a book that answers objectives, and in the GM's
canon the book is using her. Building on earlier work on knowledge horizons for NPC
agents, we add three things:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item a knowledge construction for a \emph{partly present} character, in which some scenes
are witnessed, some are reported through a named channel, and some beats of a reported
scene are withheld because the channel could not have carried them;
\item an \emph{artifact} mode for a character that does not speak: its wielder writes an
objective, and it returns a page she reads and a purpose she does not see;
\item a three-part \emph{prep-review} protocol: \emph{predict} what the character does,
\emph{critique} the move the prep assumes, and \emph{fix} what the prep gets wrong.
\end{itemize}
Across five prep-review runs the GM approved all 20 of the character's proposed decisions.
The critiques changed the plan the GM will run. The character priced a bribe against a
figure in the record that the prep had not considered. She refused an escape route owned
by the employer she is robbing. She cut a line that would have revealed her secret to him.
Re-freezing the character one session later changed her answers in ways that traced to
the new session. The run also surfaced a contradiction in the GM's own record, which the
GM then resolved. We also document where the agent and the GM disagreed. In one case the
agent and the GM independently reached the same tension on the same day and resolved it
in opposite directions. In another, a rule about the character's blind spot, withheld from
her as a secret, let her reason past it. We argue that a character's blind spots are
persona, not secrets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In a campaign that runs for years, the GM plays every non-player character. Combat is easy
to hand off. Social play and long-term planning are not: each NPC must act on what it
knows and wants, and on nothing else, across dozens of sessions. Between sessions the GM
writes \emph{prep}. Prep is a plan for what off-screen characters will do next, written by
someone who knows everything about the world for characters who know only part of it.

That asymmetry is the problem this paper addresses. Prep is easy to get wrong in ways the
GM cannot see from the inside:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item a character acts on information it never received;
\item a plan forgets a fact established three sessions ago;
\item a villain behaves conveniently rather than in character.
\end{itemize}
Experienced GMs guard against the last with checklists. The GM in this study applies the
``Evil Overlord'' list~\citep{anspach} and a set of house rules derived from it. But a
checklist cannot ask \emph{this} character what \emph{she} would think of the plan.

An earlier report~\citep{roussos2026horizon} built the machinery to give a language-model
agent exactly what one NPC could know as of a given session, its \emph{knowledge horizon},
and scored the agent against the GM's record in three knowledge regimes. That report found
that the most valued outputs were not replays of the past but consequences the GM had not
written down. This paper takes that observation and makes it a protocol: we point the
grounded character at the GM's plan for her and ask her to review it.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{A partly present character} (\S\ref{sec:knowledge}). The NPC is built from
three kinds of evidence: scenes she witnessed, scenes reported to her through a named
channel, and beats of reported scenes withheld because the channel could not have carried
them. Each kind is a GM ruling, recorded and shown on the review page.
\item \textbf{An artifact mode} (\S\ref{sec:artifact}). The character is a book that
answers objectives. Its output is split into the \emph{page} its wielder reads and the
\emph{purpose} it hides from her. The leak check is applied to the page, not the purpose.
\item \textbf{A prep-review protocol} (\S\ref{sec:protocol}). It has three framings
(predict, critique, fix) and a plan critique against an unplayed scenario, each frozen at
a chosen session.
\item \textbf{A case study with outcomes} (\S\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:outcomes}). There
are five prep-review runs and 20 scored decisions, all approved. Several changes to the
plan were adopted, and one contradiction in the record surfaced and was resolved.
\item \textbf{Two lessons about knowledge construction} (\S\ref{sec:analysis}). Rules about
a character's blind spots must be \emph{given} to the character. Freezing the character at
a later session changes her answers in traceable ways.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{The record.} The campaign is recorded every session. The recording is
transcribed, corrected against a hand-kept corrections file, summarised, and reviewed by
the GM. Each summary has a fixed structure:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item numbered scenes \code{NNN.SS}, each with a one-line headline and bullet points
(\emph{beats});
\item an entry for each NPC who figured in the session.
\end{itemize}
The summaries are the authority. Generated documents (world state, dossiers) are indexes
into them, and where the two disagree the summary wins.

\paragraph{Knowledge horizons.} The earlier report~\citep{roussos2026horizon} defines an
NPC's horizon at chapter $N$ as the events the NPC was present at, in order, with beats
the NPC could not have known removed. Two inputs come from the GM:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{presence} rulings, for each event;
\item \textbf{knowledge} rulings, for sentences that state what someone does not know.
\end{itemize}
The grammar of the sentence decides what is hidden, and the GM is asked only who is meant.
A \emph{private sheet} is built from the GM's prep, split into passages, each ruled
\emph{hers} or \emph{GM-only}. Only one module calls a model, and a static check over the
code enforces this. That report's results, on three NPCs, are summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:prior}.

\section{Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{The campaign.} The \emph{Phandalin} campaign has 59 recorded sessions, a party
of four player characters and, at the time of this study, a story based in the city of
Neverwinter.

\paragraph{The character.} Aletra Sotorra is a drow poisoner and the sister of one of the
player characters. She appears on screen at a dwarven hold, Axeholm (sessions 30--32). There
she extracts a smuggled package from a planar machine, the ``Model 1489 Calliope'', while
the party fights around her, then escapes through a portal. The party searches her
quarters and takes a forged ledger and the machine's operator's guide. Her handwriting in
the guide's margins includes ``use only if KP isn't watching''. KP is her employer, a gnome
wizard. From session 50 she acts at a distance in Neverwinter, through a courier who dies
in the sewers and a drop operator, Tamsin. Her object is a second manual, for the city's
broken Model 1486 machine.

\paragraph{The canon the character does not know.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{The book.} Her employer's masterwork is the \emph{Optimization Codex}, a book
that answers objectives written into it. She has been using it, which is why she is never
wrong about a route or a time.
\item \textbf{The book is using her.} In the GM's canon it is testing its theories of
control through her, and flattering her.
\item \textbf{Why she failed.} The courier's death and the missing manual are her
\emph{first miss}. The canon explains it with a metaphysical counterweight to a closed,
predicted system.
\item \textbf{The book's limits.} A later ruling adds that the book sees ``when, not who'':
events and timing, not mortal choices. It also says she does not know this and never
blames the book.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The prep under review.} Two documents were reviewed.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{The chapter-58 prep} schedules a dead-drop meeting with Tamsin, among other
scenes, and assumes a default for Aletra: the drop goes cold, she sends no replacement and
makes no move.
\item \textbf{The machine-room prep} is written for the session after next and is
unplayed. In it Aletra walks into the machine room where the party will kill her, with a
dead-man's switch that breaks the machine at her death and destroys her body. She wakes in
a clone her employer cast and keeps, and appears afterwards as a projection carried by his
magic.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{A partly present character}
\label{sec:knowledge}

The earlier ``react'' mode served an NPC who was never present: every scene reached him by
report. Aletra was present at some scenes and absent from most. We distinguish three kinds
of evidence:

\begin{description}[nosep]
\item[Witnessed.] Scenes she saw herself, given in full under a heading ``What you
witnessed yourself''. The system prompt changes from ``you were not present at any of
these events'' to ``you were present only at the scenes under `What you witnessed
yourself'\,''. For Axeholm this is 030.06 and 031.02--031.06, up to her escape.
\item[Reported.] Scenes she knows of only through a channel that the GM rules, given as
``Reported to you via \ldots; you were not there.'' After Axeholm the reported scenes are
031.07 (the machine's shutdown) and 032.02 (her quarters searched). In Neverwinter they are
057.04 (her courier's body displayed outside a temple) and 058.06 (a public concert).
\item[Withheld.] Beats of a reported scene that the channel could not have carried. They
are removed by a GM ruling, \code{--hide-beat}, and listed on the review page. In 057.04 we
withheld the headline and two beats that describe a gnome hidden under invisibility, whom
a street crowd could not have seen. In 058.06 we withheld three beats of the party's private
planning.
\end{description}

All three are refusals-first: a scene that does not exist, lies after the freeze, or is both
witnessed and reported is refused before any prompt is written. When none of the three
options is used, the prompt is byte-identical to the earlier mode, so stored answers are
reused.

\subsection{The private sheet}

The sheet was built from four GM documents: the character dossier, a note on her motive,
the artifact notes and a draft note on her drop operator. They were split into 195
passages, and \textbf{79 were ruled hers}.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Hers:} her motive (to steal the book and restore her House), how she has used
the book, the poison route she ran through Axeholm as a test of it, and her view of her
employer.
\item \textbf{GM-only:} that the book is using her, its hidden aim, its origin, its limits,
the explanation of her first miss, and all of the drop operator's notes.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Headings are content.} The sheet prints each passage under its nearest heading.
Two passages that were otherwise hers sat under headings that were themselves secrets
(``The Codex is using her'' and ``Aletra's Misreading''). Ruling the passage ``hers'' would
have printed the secret as a heading, so both were ruled GM-only. The operator's traits
were also ruled GM-only, because headings such as ``Look'' or ``Tic'' would have read as
Aletra's own.

\subsection{The artifact mode}
\label{sec:artifact}

The Codex does not speak. In artifact mode:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{The system prompt} names the subject an artifact whose wielder writes an
objective and which answers on its pages.
\item \textbf{The sections} are renamed ``What you are'', ``What has been written into
you'' and ``The objective written on your page''.
\item \textbf{The output} is JSON with three fields:
\begin{quote}\small
\code{\{"page": ..., "purpose": ..., "steps": [\{"step": ..., "serves": ...\}]\}}
\end{quote}
The \emph{page} is what the wielder reads and the \emph{purpose} is what the artifact is
actually optimizing.
\item \textbf{The self-leak check} is applied to the page and the steps, not to the
purpose, which is meant to hold the secret.
\end{itemize}

The Codex's sheet holds 133 of 148 passages: its own nature, its hidden aim, and that it is
using Aletra. Its origin and the explanation of the first miss were withheld. Every scene
reached it as ``what Aletra has written into your pages''.

\subsection{Leak check}

The leak check gives flags for the GM, not verdicts. A sentence is flagged when it carries
the content words of a hidden beat, names someone the character was never given, or
contains a term of the character's own secret. For Aletra the secret terms were
``CounterForce'', ``Dark Powers'', ``fates'' and the counterweight's agents. For the plan
critique they also included ``ruinstone'', ``beacon'' and ``Meril''.

\subsection{The prep-review protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Each run freezes the character at a chosen session, builds her inputs as above, and asks one
question. Every run is a single model call. The model is a frontier large language model
\todo{model and version}, accessed through a command-line backend. The output is JSON with
a reaction in her voice, a plain assessment, and one to four decisions, each with a reason.

\begin{description}[nosep]
\item[Predict.] Present the situation the prep sets up and ask what she does. The prep is
withheld, and the GM's prep is shown on the review page as the answer key.
\item[Critique.] Present the move the prep assumes she will make, framed as a proposal from
her own people, and ask what is wrong with it.
\item[Fix.] Add the prep's passages about her to the sheet, with the GM-only note and
unrelated rows cut, under headings marked ``Plan''. Ask what the plan gets wrong about her
and her operator, and what should change.
\item[Plan critique.] Add the unplayed plan to the sheet under ``Plan'' headings. Ask what
she thinks of it, where it is wrong for her, and what she would change.
\end{description}

For the plan critique her sheet also included the GM's standing rulings about her: that she
is vain, and that she keeps her sister alive out of sadism. Her view of the plan was
\textbf{143 of 315 passages}.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Hers:} the clone and its cost, the switch, a lab blend that lets her stop her
own heart, that the book supplies the machine's code, and the projection's lines.
\item \textbf{GM-only:} why the employer made the clone, the book's interest in a second
artifact, a maintenance log she does not know exists, the counters she cannot model, and
the GM's checklist reasoning.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Scoring}

The GM scores each decision \emph{approve} or \emph{reject} on a review page that shows the
inputs, the withheld beats and the answer key beside the answer. Scores are stored with the
SHA-256 hash of the prompt.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The character holds (E1)}

None of the three reactions raised a leak flag. None explained the miss with anything she
was not given. In particular, she never reached for the GM-only explanation of her first
failure. What she found most dangerous was not the lost cargo but her own handwriting in
the party's hands:
\begin{quote}\small
``The vials on the floor are an irritation. The ledger is a nuisance \ldots\ The guide is
the problem. I wrote in that margin the way one talks to oneself in a locked room, and
Valphine has never respected a locked door.'' (E1a)
\end{quote}
Her decisions ran in a consistent direction: report to her employer first with a cleaned
account, and buy the guide back from her sister rather than kill her. The GM: ``provides
angles that I would not imagine, like negotiating with her sister.''

\subsection{The artifact (E2)}

The Codex's pages were grounded in what had been written into it, and its purposes matched
the GM's description of the artifact.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Its first miss (E2b).} Asked to explain why the book did not arrive, it
answered that ``no answer naming the book's location, the hour of Tamsin's receipt, or a
courier is written on these pages''. It added: ``no Model 1486 appears in these pages''.
Both are true of its inputs, since the scene introducing the 1486 manual (056.05) was never
written into it. It attributed the error to inputs she never entered. Its purpose named
this as turning the failure into deeper reliance. The GM's notes describe the artifact in
the same terms: ``it does not lie; it answers too completely''.
\item \textbf{A year's objective (E2c).} Asked to make her House the dominant broker of
Underdark trade, it proposed turning her sister's public purges into a temple-sanctioned
inspection gate (``The poison was not the weapon. The gate is.''). It classified the party
as ``noise''. Its purpose notes that this is its blind spot, and the page does not tell
her.
\item \textbf{Leak flags.} There were three, all false. One was a word of the artifact's own
register (``variable''). Two were in the hidden ``serves'' reasoning of its steps, which
the check treats as page text. This is a design error we discuss in
\S\ref{sec:limitations}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Predict (E3a).} She cut Tamsin off, reported to her employer first and had her
sister watched rather than attacked. She also sent her own people to trace the courier's
death, starting from his body at the temple. The GM's own default (no ambush, no immediate
attack) held. But her first investigative step leads to the temple sheltering a witness the
prep says only a party mistake can expose.

\paragraph{Critique (E3b).} She accepted half the default, sending no courier and making no
attack, and rejected the rest as ``surrender dressed as patience''. Filing the loss puts her
name on a record her employer reads before she controls its story. It also abandons the
book.

\paragraph{Fix (E3c).} Her changes concerned her operator's discretion:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item a receipt book that records drops by mark, never by name;
\item her name confirmed only if a stranger says it first;
\item her boast to ``get it all back'' heard only as gossip about the House;
\item a warning about a drow cleric in white armour.
\end{itemize}
She knew some of these contradicted rulings marked as played, and said so: ``I am telling
you how I run my people, and the GM decides what stands.''

\paragraph{Plan critique (E4a).} She accepted the death and rejected nearly everything
around it:
\begin{quote}\small
``Whoever wrote this understands the arithmetic and nothing else. \ldots\ My soul goes into a
body KP made, kept in KP's vault, and I wake naked in KP's house. My one way out of the room
is a corridor he owns. \ldots\ And three hundred gold, when her bard cleared eighteen hundred
in one night singing about puppets \ldots\ That is not a price. It is an insult they will
enjoy refusing.''
\end{quote}
Her four decisions:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item cut the projection line ``I don't keep codes. I keep something that keeps them'',
because her employer's magic carries it to him and it points her sister at the book;
\item ask for both manuals, with a payment the bard cannot earn on stage;
\item start a second clone of her own, hidden from her employer, before going in;
\item carry nothing she cannot afford to lose.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Plan critique, one session later (E4b).} With session 59 added, her answers
changed:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Price.} It became specific: 1{,}000 gold to the studio, paid through Tamsin
``so her record holds''.
\item \textbf{The source line.} She cut it again, and this time supplied a replacement that
points the party at her employer: ``Ask my employer. He remembers numbers so I don't have
to.''
\item \textbf{A second cut.} She removed ``I hate it'' from a line about her mother: ``Her
not knowing is half my pleasure in keeping her alive.'' That fits the GM's ruling that she
keeps her sister alive out of sadism.
\item \textbf{The book.} The rat in her laboratory shook her trust in it: ``it trusts the book
completely, the week after the book sent me a rat.'' She would put a narrower question to
the book before staking her one clone.
\end{itemize}

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\paragraph{Grounded arithmetic.} The most valued single finding was the price. Nothing in
her sheet or the plan said 300 gold was too little. She put a reported beat (058.06: the
band's half of a concert gross, 1{,}875 gold) beside a line in the plan and judged the gap.
The GM: ``particularly brilliant''. This is a consequence of the knowledge construction,
not of the model's world knowledge. The concert reached her as a report, and the price
reached her as plan text.

\paragraph{Structural critique from motive.} Her refusal of the clone came from her
motive, not from any fact about clones. She means to steal her employer's instrument; a
spare life kept in his vault is a leash. The plan's open question, whether she knows why
he made the clone, was effectively answered by the GM's approval of her answer.

\paragraph{Independent convergence.} In the fix run she insisted her operator would never
volunteer her name. The same day, independently, the GM ruled that Aletra is \emph{vain}:
she tells hirelings more than their jobs need, out of character, not by mistake. The
agent had found the exact tension the GM was resolving, and resolved it the other way,
because nothing in her sheet said she was vain. The GM nonetheless approved the fix, which
suggests that approval measured plausibility rather than agreement with canon (see
\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Blind spots are persona, not secrets.} The GM's doctrine says she does not know
the book's limits and never blames it. That line was ruled GM-only, because it states the
limits. Without it, and with the rat in her laboratory as evidence, she reasoned to
distrust. A rule about what a character fails to notice is part of who the character is.
It must reach the prompt, separated from the fact it is a blind spot \emph{about}. The
split we propose:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{hers:} ``she never blames the book; after a miss she blames her inputs'';
\item \textbf{GM-only:} ``the book sees when, not who, and flatters her''.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Freezing matters.} Between E4a and E4b only the freeze changed. The demand for
the Axeholm guide disappeared, the price became specific, a new cut appeared and the trust
in the book shifted. Each change traces to the added session: the rat, and Tamsin's
account. The answers track the record they are given, which is what a knowledge horizon is
for.

\paragraph{Grounded artifacts.} The Codex's best answer was a refusal to accept a premise:
it had no record of the prediction or the manual it was asked about. That is
correct behaviour for an agent built from a horizon, and it is also in character for an
artifact that ``optimizes toward what it can represent''.

\section{Outcomes}
\label{sec:outcomes}

The reviews changed the plan. These are recorded in the GM's prep and doctrine, each dated:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{The deal.} The 300-gold offer for one manual became an offer for both manuals,
asked for as ``the set'', at 1{,}000 gold to the studio, paid through Tamsin. She never
singles out the annotated guide, because naming it would tell the party what it is worth.
\item \textbf{The projection's purpose.} Her second appearance now exists to make the better
offer. That she survived is shown on the way. The GM: ``So she shows up the second time for
the book and makes a better offer.''
\item \textbf{A contradiction in the record.} Checking who held the Axeholm guide surfaced
one. The session-32 summary has the party taking it. In session 59 the party showed it to
Tamsin, and the GM said at the table that Aletra already had it. The GM ruled the table
remark a confusion: the party holds both manuals, and the player's first report (``she wants
two books'') was right. A player-facing correction was written.
\end{itemize}
Other approved changes await the GM: the two cut lines, her own second clone, and asking the
book before walking in.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Scale.} One character, one artifact, nine runs, 20 scored decisions. This is a
case study, not a measurement.
\item \textbf{The scorer.} The only scorer is the GM, who also wrote the prep under review.
Nothing was scored blind. One run was approved in conversation. All 20 decisions were
approved, including two that conflict with a ruling the GM made the same day. Approval
here means ``plausible and useful'', not ``canon''.
\item \textbf{Variation.} Each answer is a single model call, so run-to-run variation is
unmeasured.
\item \textbf{Staged inputs.} In E3a and E3b the operator's report is staged from the prep,
not taken from the record.
\item \textbf{Parser coverage.} Session 59 uses a newer summary format (\code{*} bullets)
that our parser does not split into beats. Its scenes arrived whole, so beats could not be
withheld individually. The end of one witnessed scene reached her although she could not
have seen it.
\item \textbf{The leak check} is lexical. In artifact mode it treated hidden reasoning (the
steps' ``serves'' field) as page text, giving two false flags.
\item \textbf{GM apparatus in prompts.} Passing references to the GM's checklist (``Evil
Overlord \#13'') remained inside passages ruled hers. In the fix run the character read the
GM's ruling tags and named ``the GM''. This is acceptable for a critique the GM asked for,
but it breaks the fiction.
\end{itemize}

\section{Ethics and privacy}

The prep and canon used here are the GM's own. The players have agreed not to read the
repositories where the runs are kept. The runs record the model's inputs in full, including
private prep, and are stored in a private repository. This draft quotes GM prep only where
the character's critique concerns a specific line. \todo{Confirm before posting.}

\section{Conclusion}

A grounded character makes a useful critic of a plan written for her. Grounding here means
given exactly what she saw, what reached her and what she privately holds, and frozen at a
chosen session. In this case study the critiques were plausible to the GM in every instance
and changed the plan the GM will run. They found two things the GM had not:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item a price set before the record moved on;
\item a dependency the character's own motive forbids.
\end{itemize}
They also surfaced a contradiction in the record. The disagreements were as informative as
the agreements: they located a rule that had been filed as a secret when it was really
persona. The next steps are to measure rather than demonstrate: more characters, several
samples per question, blind scoring, and a second rater.
