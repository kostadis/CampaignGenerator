# Chapter 51: We're famous. Why are we famous?

## Vukradin — Patrons of the Chord

The Common Chord used to throw me out for not paying. Now it is the hardest ticket in Neverwinter, and I am inside it on a weeknight with a table of my own, and the man at the next table is explaining to his friend, loudly, over a plate of food that would have cost my entire first week in this city, that the Chord has become unaffordable and it is entirely the fault of Vukradin.

He has not turned around. He does not know I am here. I let him keep going, because a man should be allowed to finish a thought.

"Aldus still hasn't given us a time," Soma says, from across our table, working a bit of bread between her fingers. "For the dinner."

"Then Lord Neverember can wait." I say it lightly, and then I hear myself and stop. "Neverember. Not Neverwinter. Neverember. It's never a fire ember? Like, the ember is never—"

"He's never flaming," Soma says. "Keeps it very quiet."

"Yes. That. Soma has it exactly right." I love this city's names. I do not love what they do to a sentence. "Anyway. Lord Neverflaming has, according to the rumor, a new drow advisor."

"Isn't that Valphine?" Soma says, and then, a half-beat later, at Valphine's face, "Ow. Right. No."

Valphine has been listening to the room the way she listens to everything, which is to say with the patience of a person counting knives, and she surfaces now. "Which we assume to be one of my siblings, in fact."

"That is my guess too." It is a rumor. I hold it as a rumor. But I suspect that when we finally sit down to that dinner, Valphine is going to look across the table at somebody and know exactly which house they grew up in, and I would very much like to be watching her face when it happens. Everyone in this world is related to everyone. I have stopped being surprised.

"And Brewbarry's bathrobe business," I say, because I keep the list in my head and this is on the list. "Have we secured the capital, or is that still—"

"We have the money," Soma says. "It's the imports. The shipping lanes—portals, whatever—are still hosed."

"And House Margaster runs what's left of the logistics."

"Of course they do."

Here is the thing I keep having to admit about House Margaster, and it costs me every time: they deliver on time and on schedule. I have said the name out loud as a curse. I have watched what their money does. And the crates still arrive when the manifest says they will. I do not know how to make those two facts sing in the same key, so I leave them side by side and let them be dissonant.

"Valphine," I say. "What's your read on Margaster? Impressed? Unimpressed?"

She considers the wineglass she has not touched. "Competition," she says.

That is a complete answer, from her. I have learned not to ask for the second verse.

"Alright. Lim's food deliveries by three in the morning. We get there, we cast whatever we need to cast, we're ready." Three in the morning is low tide, and low tide matters, and I know it matters because of what I overhear next—not because anyone tells me, but because the Chord is a room full of people who talk with their mouths full and I am a person who listens. Somebody two tables over is complaining about the sewers, and the sewers, it turns out, have a tide. The river comes up. The sea pushes in.

"A river has a tide?" I say, to nobody, and then to my table: "How many moons does this planet have?"

"That's an easy question," says Valphine, who is now being drawn into a conversation I can see she did not want. "One. Selûne."

"Selûne. Of course." I knew that. I have written about that moon. "And the Tears of Selûne, so—two tidal bodies, there should be two—"

"The Tears are rock," Valphine says. "A cluster. Not enough mass to move water."

"Comets," Soma offers, unhelpfully.

"Asteroids. Not really moons. Fine. One moon." I do the arithmetic while Soma looks at me the way she looks at children stacking cups. "Two high, two low. So it's a six-hour swing, high to low—no—yes. Six hours. We want to be under at the bottom of it."

"Three in the morning," Soma says.

"Which gives us three hours, maybe four, before it gets genuinely unpleasant."

"I can sit underwater indefinitely," Soma says, and returns to her bread.

Which brings me back to the man at the next table, who has now moved from *the Chord is unaffordable* to *and Petra's turned it into a racket*, which, honestly, I have thoughts about too, and I stand up, because there is a solution to his problem, and the solution is me.

"Excuse me. I couldn't help overhearing." His friend's face does the thing faces do. His own takes a second longer, because he is a few cups along. "I'm actually planning on opening my own independent music studio. Low-cost. Nonprofit. A place where you could hear Lathander's Death on a regular basis without—" I gesture at the room, the leather, the food. "This. And if you'd like to contribute to the charitable fund, that speeds the opening. And spread the word. All the usual ways. Sending stone. Whatever's convenient."

He looks at me for a long time, in the manner of a drunk man deciding whether he is being insulted, and then he says, "Man, I don't have a lot of money," and puts ten gold pieces in my hand.

Ten. From a random nobody at the next table.

"That's—wow. That's pretty generous, actually."

"Hell of a donation," Soma says behind me.

"That goes in the studio fund. Not the general coffers. The studio fund." I am already keeping the two ledgers separate in my head; I have been keeping them separate for months. "Thank you. Thank you very much. That's awesome."

He is not done. "Do I get—is there a sign? For people who contributed?"

"Absolutely. Yes." And I hear myself building the structure as I speak, the way a chord builds out of the first note you strike: "There are different tiers of giving, actually. Ten gold pieces puts you at—"

"Premier or Plus?" Soma calls. "Which one?"

"Plus. You're at the Plus level. Not quite Premier yet. But ten gold is a lot. You should feel good about Plus." He does feel good about Plus. I can see it land. "We need your name. For the program notes. It's per year—if you want to be in next year's program you'd have to give again, but a year is a long time."

"So it's patronage," Valphine says drily, from the table.

"It's *my* patronage. Exactly. This is where the word comes from."

We have hours before three. I look around the room, at the hardest ticket in Neverwinter, at all these people who paid to be here, and I think: how many of them would rather pay to build something?

I hit up five more.

The first one knows who I am on sight—the real Vukradin, not the story—and tells me kindly that he has already done his charitable giving for this year. "That's fine. I'm not here to pressure anybody. The benefits are all on you. Plus, Premier, nothing, I'm cool. I just like fans."

"Biggest fan," he says. "Biggest fan."

"Bigger than Brewbarry?"

"Nobody is as big as Brewbarry," Valphine says from behind me, and that's just how it is.

The second is a man named Wick, who has never heard of Lathander's Death, does not believe for one second that I am Vukradin, and does not care in the slightest about either fact, because Wick is *into* patronage of the arts, and Wick thinks it is wonderful that somebody is putting together a music school.

"School?" Soma says, when I report back.

"Studio, school. If you have a studio you're training people. It's the same thing." It is not quite the same thing. Wick's gold is in the fund. We will discuss the curriculum later.

The third is Sabbatha, who gives, and who spends the entire transaction looking past me at Brewbarry, and who says, with real admiration, that she has rarely seen anyone so *committed* to a look.

"He is quite bulging, isn't he," I say.

"Rippling," Soma adds.

"Exactly."

The fourth is Ondrelle Kass. Into the notes she goes.

The fifth is Old Hesp, and Old Hesp recognizes me before I open my mouth, and what he says is: "I remember when you used to play to an empty room."

"Yeah? Did you come? Did I give you your money back at the end?"

"You did. You said if I didn't rate it ten out of ten you wouldn't take my coin."

"That's how honest I am." I mean it. I still mean it. "Well. What do you think now?"

He looks around the room the way I looked around it a minute ago, and he takes his time. "Now this is a friggin' expensive place to be. Everybody's gotta look cool. It doesn't have the look. That feel. That gritty—"

"We're going to get it back. Come to the new studio when it's running. We're going to get it back."

"It's too commercial." He is not wrong. "Petra's turned it into a money-making operation, and, look, I gotta respect her. She built it. But I remember when that table"—he nods at Brewbarry's table, the one with the leather and the shine and the little plaque—"was just a table. And they didn't have this kind of fancy food."

He gives anyway. Wick, Sabbatha, Old Hesp, Ondrelle Kass. I write them all down. Twenty gold pieces for the studio in one evening, from people who did not have to give it, which is exactly the kind of gold I have been trying to explain to my friends for a year now, and none of it stolen, none of it plundered, none of it Margaster's. Petra's five hundred for the likeness is still pending—that comes after the performance, two days out—but that is a different ledger, and this twenty is mine.

Old Hesp is right about the Chord, though. It has stopped being a room where music happens and started being a room where money happens near music. I did that. Not on purpose, but I did it. That is a note I will have to resolve later.

"Alright," I say to my table, and pocket the list. "Let's go do some spelunking."

## Vukradin — Nine Crates at Low Tide

The grill sits at the bottom of a run of wet stone steps that look like somebody once meant them for an aqueduct and then forgot. It is nearly three. The tide is out. You can hear it being out—a low, patient sucking somewhere down in the dark, water leaving stone the way it does—and above us the city is as quiet as Neverwinter ever gets.

"I cast *Find Familiar*," Soma says, and a rat arrives, sleek and grey and much more composed than any rat has a right to be, and sits on her shoulder like it has been told it is going to be important.

"We need someone to do something stealthily," I say. "I have a very high stealth, if we need me to—"

"I didn't know bards got high stealth."

"It's the boots. Boots of elvenkind. When I move quietly, I move *very* quietly." I say it as a fact, because it is a fact, and I do not understand why Soma's face does what it does.

"Hell yeah," she says, eventually.

They come at the third hour, like the chits will later tell us they always do, though we do not know about the chits yet. Two men. Nine crates between them, down the wet steps, and I count them, and Soma counts them, because nine is the number Lim's whole shady arrangement turns on. Eight of the crates have no Commission Harbor mark anywhere on them, which we knew—which is the entire point—but there is something about watching unmarked goods come down a stairwell at three in the morning that turns a thing you knew into a thing you have seen.

Eight stacked together, neat, for Lim. The ninth set apart on a ledge.

The man who sets the ninth one down stops. He looks down into the dark behind the grill—looks *for* something, the way you look down a road for a coach that's late—and then he straightens and walks away. The other man just stacks boxes. That is all he does. And when they reach the top of the steps they go in different directions, and they do not look at each other, and they do not say goodbye, and it is so careful that it is loud.

"That nonchalant whistling," Soma murmurs.

"If they split, that makes them hard to follow. You'll have to pick one."

Valphine has been watching them the whole time with that flat archivist's attention. She takes a moment, and I give her the last of what I have—one more bardic push, the final one of the night—because this is the kind of choice you do not want to make at half strength. "The one who looked into the sewer," she says. "He knows what's coming out of it. The other one is a pair of arms."

"So we want two things," I say. "One: someone tracks whoever comes for crate nine. Two: someone tracks the men. Two things, two groups."

"I'm tracking the crate," Soma says. "The ninth one. The one that doesn't go to Lim. I'm more concerned about my friend's shady business than about a man with a box."

"Okay, so you want me to follow the men back through the sewers? Alone? That seems—"

"No—fine. We send the rat that way. You take the crate."

"Vukradin is going to stick around, stealthily, and wait to see who comes for the ninth crate, and then track it." It is easier to say a plan out loud as if it is already happening. "Lim comes out, takes eight. Somebody else takes one. I follow the one."

"So everyone but Vukradin trails the men," Valphine says, "and Vukradin trails the crate. Is that right?"

"Yes."

"Yes," says Soma.

We wait. Soma's rat waits. And then something comes up through the grill for the ninth crate, and it is not a person.

It is small. It is quick. It is cat-sized and made of brass and leather and it moves with the unhurried competence of a thing following instructions—not sneaking, not hurrying, just *doing*, the way a good stagehand moves in the dark between numbers. It picks up the crate. It goes back down.

"Okay," I say. "Vukradin will pursue. Stealthily."

"This is my concern," Soma says, and she is not joking now, or she is joking the way she jokes about things she means. "Vukradin ends up going into the sewers. One person. In the sewers."

"I'll be fine."

"Tell me the odds."

I have actually done this arithmetic, because I do this arithmetic. Advantage on the boots. The bonus. The floor of what can go wrong. "Only about a one in four hundred chance of disaster. It'll be fine."

"Alright," she says. "Alright."

"Soma—you've got one Sending Stone. I've got the other."

"Good to know. Walkie-talkies."

"We have walkie-talkies."

And I go down.

Under the grill the dark is total and the stone is slick and the air is the air of a place the tide just left. I keep the construct's footfalls ahead of me—a soft, regular tick of brass on wet stone, no wasted motion in it—and I match it, quiet as the boots make me, which is very quiet indeed. This is, I think, the most competent I have ever felt. I could do this for a living. I could open a stealthy-pursuit consultancy. Fair rates.

The stone in my pocket warms, and Soma's voice comes through it, low and dry: the man they picked has stopped at a cook shop. One of maybe three in the city open at the fourth hour. He is eating. Alone. Not talking to anyone.

"Is he eating turtle soup at this hour?" she says, to someone on her side, and then, to me, "He's not at Lim's. Somewhere else."

"Remember," I whisper into the stone, "we promised Lim we're not going to spook her connections." I catch myself. "Or *you* promised her. Whichever."

"Thank you so much," says the stone. "I'm not trying to put her out of business. I want to see where he goes next. A snack tells us nothing."

Which is fine, and correct, and I would say so, except that somewhere in the next fifty feet of tunnel something happens to me that I do not have a good word for.

It is not fear. I know what fear does to me; fear makes me quiet. This is not that. It is more like—the tunnel ahead becomes *boring*. Not dangerous. Not wrong. Just utterly, profoundly not worth my time. The brass ticking goes on ahead and I find myself thinking, with total conviction, that following it is the dullest possible way to spend the next hour, that this is not the right way, that nobody would blame me for turning around, that there is really nothing down there. It is the feeling of a song you have heard too many times. It is the feeling of a rehearsal that should have ended twenty minutes ago.

So I turn around. Not confused—I know exactly why I came down here, I know there is a ninth crate and a construct and a question I want answered. I simply, for the first time in my life, do not care about the answer.

It takes me a long time to notice that this is not a feeling I have ever had before. By the time I notice, I have walked a long way in the wrong direction, and the ticking is gone.

"Okay," I say aloud, to the dark, in the register I use when something has been done to me and I am cataloguing it. "There's some kind of passive effect down here. An area effect. Nobody cast that on me. That was *in the tunnel*."

The dark does not disagree. Somewhere far behind me, the tide is beginning to think about coming back.

## Vukradin — Lost in Rsolk's Tunnels

The first thing I see of them is a lantern, and the second is a man standing in front of a knot of other men, and the third is the man's face when he sees me, which is not the face of someone who is about to hurt me. It is the face of someone who has just found a goat in his kitchen.

"How the hell did you get over here?"

"I don't know," I say, which is true. "Where am I?"

He looks at me. He looks at the men behind him. He looks back at me.

"Because I'm not over there," I add, helpfully, and I can hear how it sounds.

I try to read him and get nothing. I am not good at reading men in tunnels; I am good at reading rooms with a stage in them. What I get instead is a vague sense that he is not angry, exactly. He is *confused*. He does not understand what I am doing here, which is fair, because neither do I—I know I came down after a crate and a brass cat, and I know some part of the tunnel behind me decided on my behalf that I didn't want to, and beyond that the map is blank.

So I do the thing I can do. I convince him I am lost. It is not a difficult performance, because I am.

"I'm lost. I don't know how I got here. I just—wrong turn somewhere." I give him the smile I give to landlords.

He looks at me, and then, deliberately, he licks his finger, as though testing which way the air is moving. "Well," he says. "This is my territory."

"This is your territory."

"Yes. This is my territory."

"Okay. Did you want me to go?"

"I'd want to know why you're in my territory."

"I'm sorry I got lost. I'm happy to leave. Could you point me toward Lim's? The café. That's where I'm trying to get."

The finger comes down. "Lim's Café?"

"Yeah. We're friends."

I watch that land wrong. Whatever I was to him a moment ago—a goat, a mistake—I am now a *question*, and the men behind him have gone still, and he is looking at me the way you look at a stranger who knows a name he shouldn't. He is toying with something. I can see it. Whether to do something about me.

And then his face changes again, and this time it goes somewhere I know.

"Are you that singer?"

"Exactly," I say, and the relief comes out as brightness. "And Lim's a fan."

"What are you *doing* down here, man?"

"I told you! I got lost!"

"You're Vukradin. You're Vukradin, right?" And behind him one of the others has started to smile—genuinely, all the way up—and he says, "Is this—is this really Vukradin?" like a man who has just been handed a good day for no reason.

"Do you guys want autographs?"

They would love autographs.

"Sure. We can do some autographs. But then I've got to be going. If you could just point me—"

"You know what," the leader says, "we're going to do one better. We're going to take you there. We don't want you getting lost again, Mr. Vukradin."

"Lim will be thrilled."

He pauses, then, and the smile thins out a little. "It's very dangerous down here."

"Is it? In *your* territory? I would think you'd keep it very safe. You seem like a safe individual. I'm sure you keep the place shipshape."

"Mr. Vukradin," he says, "I agree with you." And I think: fantastic. "But I have to tell you why I was out here. I was concerned when I saw you, and I'm very glad I recognized you. One of my guys tells me you've got a deal with a friend of mine."

"At the Common Chord?"

"No, no. The Toe mine."

"Mountain's Toe. Oh—Zeleen? I didn't know you knew him."

"Oh, yeah. He said he had a business relationship with you. I told him he was full of shit."

"No, that's true. That's true. They haven't produced very much yet, but they say they're working hard."

He laughs. It is a real laugh, out loud, in a sewer. "That's how I feel about it too," I tell him, and he laughs again, and it goes on long enough that I start to like him, which I am aware is a thing that happens to me.

"Look," he says, still laughing. "I manage the tunnels here."

"Oh. You're the tunnel manager."

"Yeah. A tunnel manager."

"So all of this—all the tunnels—"

"Not all the tunnels. The tunnels in this area."

"The tunnels in this area. Interesting. Okay." His name is Rsolk, it turns out, and he says it the way a man says a name he expects people to already know, and I file it next to *tunnel manager* and *knows Lim* and *knows Zeleen* and a private, un-provable suspicion about exactly what sort of men live in Neverwinter's sewers and call it territory. I do not say the suspicion out loud. I have been wrong about men in tunnels before.

"Two months ago," Rsolk says, "things started coming up the floodline."

"Floodline."

He describes a tunnel that floods regular as the tide, and I say, honestly, "I don't know anything about tunnels, man, I'm a musician," and he goes on anyway. "I've been having trouble there. I've lost men. So we don't go there anymore."

And there it is. I hear it the way you hear the key change coming two bars before it arrives. "Do you need help with that? My friends and I have some abilities. And we're looking for ways to make money. Maybe we can help you."

He wants it. He wants it so badly he does not bother to pretend. "One of the guys who got out," he says, "said there was some kind of creature. Something they'd never seen."

"Oh."

"It looked like a spider. But it had all the wrong colors. And the webs—the webs were all wrong. There was something really off about them."

I know that spider. Or I know its cousin. We have stood in webbing that was the wrong texture and the wrong sheen and did not belong to anything that should exist, and I remember how it felt on my hands, and I remember how the fight went. "Yeah," I say. "We have experience with that."

He does not ask for a rate. What he asks, carefully, like a man reaching for something on a high shelf, is: "Mr. Vukradin, I know this is too much to ask. But could you get me some tickets to the Common Chord?"

Of all the payment I have ever been offered underground.

"I can ask. We have—kind of an arm's-length relationship with the Chord right now. But I will ask for you."

"That's all I could ask for."

"Alright. Let's do some autographs, and then we've got to be on our way."

I sign for all of them. The one who smiled first holds his out like it might break. And then Rsolk's men close around me, not unkindly, and walk me back through their territory toward stone I recognize, and I think: clean work. Monsters that are killing people, for a man who will pay in tickets and gratitude and whatever the tunnels turn up. Nobody's blood on the coin. Conflict-free.

Fair trade gold, from a sewer. This city.

## Soma — Denvar's Unsettled Account

Dinner at four in the morning is breakfast. I keep forgetting that about people who work nights.

He eats it like a man who is not worried about the price. Alone. Back to the wall, but not watching the door—just eating. Valphine has been looking at him across the cook shop the way she looks at a lock, and when I raise my brow at her she shakes her head a fraction. "He's what he appears to be. A man who carries things. Not a face on someone else's mask."

Good. Masks are tiring.

He walks home with his belly full and we walk behind him, far enough back, Brewbarry doing his best not to be the size he is. Bluelake District. The building is one of those tall narrow warrens with a door every few steps down a hall—one room to a person, a landlord on a stool at the bottom, a smell of other people's cooking. High-efficiency flat, they call it here. Back home we'd have called it a rookery. He goes up, goes in, and the small sounds through the wall say he is asleep inside a quarter hour.

I send the rat in under the door.

It is a good body for this. Low, quick, and nobody looks at a rat in a building like this because there are always rats in a building like this. It noses along the baseboard and up onto the chair and along the shelf while he sleeps, and I ride behind its eyes and its whiskers and look for the things I told it to look for. Odd signs. Receipts. Pay stubs. A badge, a mark, a scrap of anything with a house on it.

There is nothing on the walls. Nothing sewn into the coat. No insignia, no colors, nothing for a rat to smell out. What there is, instead, is a room that is too nice for a man who carries boxes. Boots with good soles. A second coat. A blanket that isn't army surplus. And he eats out at four in the morning, which a day laborer does not do, because a day laborer at four in the morning is asleep or hungry.

"He's a little overcompensated for his job," I say, low, to Valphine.

"Yes."

"I want that gig," I add, before I remember what the gig probably is.

"He has a landlord," Valphine says. "Who we could ask questions of."

The landlord is right there, on his stool at the bottom of the stairs, and he has been looking at us since we came in, and there is no way to ask about a tenant quietly in a building where the walls are made of paper and everyone can hear you breathe. So I do the thing I do. I walk up to him and I ask.

"We just had some questions. What's it cost to live here? I think that's the first one."

He looks at me. He takes his time about it. "I don't see many turtles around here."

"That's not what I asked."

"I'm not being—look. I thought turtles carried their house on their shoulders. So they didn't need a place to stay."

I have lived a long time and I have heard most things a human can say about a shell. This one is new. "I see what you think of us. I can't *live* in this thing. It merely protects me." I let him hear the weight of it. "I have a cabin, actually. Out in the woods. Outside Neverwinter—not in the city proper."

"Oh. Well. I learned a new thing. I didn't know turtles didn't live in their shells." He scratches his jaw. "I guess you're not like turtles."

"That's kind of slanderous to us, actually."

And then he sees Valphine, in her white armor, and he sees Brewbarry, who is a great deal of Brewbarry in a small hallway, and something in his face does a quick sum and comes up short. "I'm just asking a question."

"Oh," I say. "Just asking questions. I see."

"Just asking questions."

"Look." I hold up a hand. "I'll pretend I didn't hear some of that. I want to know what it costs to live here and what kind of people stay in a place like this. You could say I'm looking ahead. For my shell sprouts." Which is a lie, and one I tell with a straight face, because it is the kind of lie a landlord understands.

"Look, first of all, we don't care. We're an equal-opportunity housing establishment."

"So progressive."

"It isn't about being progressive. Money's money, man. I don't care who's got it." He says it like a creed. Then he turns to Valphine, because he is a man who cannot leave a thing alone. "You're a drow."

"Very astute," says Valphine.

"I thought drow didn't like sunlight."

"Oh, I love the sunlight." A pause. "I *love* the sunlight."

Every conversation with this man is about somebody's race and he does not think of himself as that kind of man. I am not going to be the one to explain it to him. It isn't our duty. He can put in the work.

"So you take money and don't ask questions," I say. "You know nothing about your tenants?"

"How do you manage the compliance," Valphine murmurs, to no one, and I let it go past.

"This is the kind of place where you'd better have the cash up front," he says, which is more useful than anything else he's told us. "It isn't the kind of place a day laborer lives."

No. It is not. "You never check? Never make sure people have the gold they say they'll have in a month?"

"I don't scry on my customers."

"I don't believe that. You scry on *some* of them."

His eyes go to Brewbarry again, and to what Brewbarry's chest is doing under his shirt, and I add, quietly, "Jiggling about," and he swallows.

"Look. I don't want any trouble. If you want a place to stay here, I'll give you a discount."

"Really, we just want to know about one of your tenants. The gentleman we followed—the one who just came in."

"You mean Denvar?"

There it is. "Denvar. Yes." A name. We came in with a man and a pouch of chits and we leave with a name, and that is more than nothing. "He dropped something last night. We saw him in an alley, very late, skulking about, and he dropped a little pouch. We'd like to return it."

"Making a delivery," Valphine adds, smooth.

And while I am telling this lie, the rat upstairs has found the chits.

They are in a stack, in order, under the good coat. Seven to ten days of them, all the same hand, all the same words. I read them through the rat's small eyes one at a time and they all say the same thing. *9 crates, 3rd hour, 8 up, 1 down.*

"Eight up, one down," I murmur, and Valphine's head turns. "Every one. He has one job. He has literally one job."

There is a client reference on each. A house mark. No name anywhere, on any of them. Weekly settlements—the stamps are there, the little check of somebody's tally, paid, paid, paid, paid, paid, paid. All of them clearly one contract, week after week, at the third hour, eight up for Lim and one down into the dark.

All of them settled but the newest. The seventh. No stamp. That one is tonight's. That one he just did.

"So that's the one he hasn't been paid for yet," I say. "So we have a reason to follow him when he wakes up, don't we."

"He needs the money," Valphine says. "For this."

"He needs it quickly." I turn back to the landlord. "Is Denvar settled up? On his bills?"

"Well," he says, "he owes me some money today."

"Yeah?"

"He's got until the end of the day. He's a good customer. Quiet. Doesn't bother anybody."

"I'll bet."

"Good guy, huh," says Valphine.

"Yeah. Totally good guy."

"You don't think he's a serial killer or anything," I say.

He looks at me—at Meril's staff in my hand—and at Valphine in her white with her eyes doing what they do in a dim hall, and at Brewbarry with the halberd he did not have anywhere to put down. "Should I be worried?"

"You never know with these types. You never know."

"I'd be worried about him," Valphine says, meaning Denvar, and lets him decide.

He is quiet a moment. Then, awkwardly, like a man who has decided to learn something: "Soma, right? You said. Now that I know turtles are looking for housing—what makes a good turtle room?"

I could tell him anything. A pit of sand. A ceiling that leaks. He would write it down.

"A nice hearth," I say. "Plenty of water. Being near a body of water is nice."

He nods and writes it down.

## Soma — Harpers Behind the Wall

Several hours in a hallway that smells of other people's cabbage is long enough for the rat to give out, so I bring it back—another slot, another rat, same grey coat, same air of being the only professional in the room—and by then Vukradin has found us. He looks like a man who has been underground. He looks, more to the point, like a man who has been *signing things* underground.

"You've been giving me updates through the stone," he says. "I figured I should join you."

"I can tell you about Denvar."

"Why don't I tell you about the local wererat mafia?"

He says it flat, like a menu. I do not ask how sure he is. "Wererats," I say. "There's wererats all over, isn't there. I expect them to be such a big player."

"Spiders, Soma. In his tunnels. Wrong colors, wrong webs—the same wrongness. And he wants us to clear them out. We clear out the spiders and it's more money for the studio. This is great. Finally a business opportunity." He says the last part to the hallway at large. "Fair Trade Gold."

Denvar comes down the stairs while Vukradin is still glowing about it. He does not look at us. Nobody looks at anybody in a building like this.

We follow him a few streets to a plain stone wall on the back of a bigger building, and there he does the thing careful men do badly: he looks around. Discreetly, he thinks. Then he works a stone loose—there is a hollow behind it—takes out a bag, seats the stone back, and stands a moment looking at the chit in his hand.

He smiles at it.

"Payday," I murmur. "Off to the casino."

"It's a dead drop," Vukradin says. "Is it a two-way thing? Like a transfer window—you put it in one side, close the door—"

"We'd have to inspect it."

"Alright. Let him go. We inspect."

Denvar goes. He walks like a man who has just paid his rent in his head. "He might be a dead end," I say, watching him turn the corner. "Unless we're back at his door before he leaves tomorrow, and we don't know how early that is."

"Eyeball the wall," I tell Valphine, and she gives the stone a long look, and the wall a longer one, and says what I already suspect: the hollow goes through. Two-way. Someone put the payment in there. Whether they did it from this side of the wall or from inside the building, you cannot tell from here.

"Let's loop around," she says.

"Or—is it earth, or—"

"Stone," says Valphine. "The whole building. Built out of stone."

"I have *Mold Earth*," I say to Vukradin, "but I know you won't."

"It's stone."

"Yeah, yeah."

Around the corner the plain wall stops being plain. There is a door and a sign and the sign says *The House of a Thousand Faces*, and it is a tavern, and it is *open*, at five in the morning, in the Bluelake District. Lamplight behind the glass. Somebody moving inside.

"Somebody's obviously in there paying off shady debts," I say.

"So many taverns," says Vukradin. "It's five o'clock—"

"—somewhere."

And Brewbarry, who has said nothing all night, looks at the door for a moment with an expression I have not seen on him before—something old, something to do with being new in a city with no coin—and walks straight in.

We let him. He has heard of this place, it turns out; back when he first came to Neverwinter this was the tavern he could not afford, and now he can. A man of class. He can buy his way into high society. "Isn't it obvious?" Valphine says to Vukradin, perfectly deadpan. "The quality of your talent."

"Not."

"It's totally not, you're sure."

"It definitely is not." He is not joking now. "There's a force behind our fame. Some group is maneuvering—we're being—"

"Manipulated," I say.

"—to their advantage. For some reason."

Yes. We have known that for a while. We are famous. We do not know why we are famous. That is a thing you keep in your shell and carry around until somebody explains it.

Vukradin has an idea, and it is a spell. We open our side of the drop, the way Denvar did; the inner closure we can't reach, but he thinks *Knock* would pop it. And then my rat goes through and has a look at whatever room is on the far side.

"How does that work? You use *Knock* on a hole in a wall?"

"We can't physically open the other side. I think we could open it magically."

"If you propose it, it's worth a shot. I like the combination."

Valphine has a different idea, and it is a business proposal: walk in, offer them a booking, get Vukradin backstage where the staff go and the paperwork lives, and call whatever he finds there *music preparation*.

"We could cast two spells," Vukradin says, weighing it, "or we could have an extended contract process, several performance roles, hours of—" He stops. "I do like to perform, though."

"Or we get paid," I point out.

"These are tough choices," I say. "Let's do both." Why not both. We want to at least not walk into a Harper's—into *anyone's*—house completely blind.

So Vukradin works the stone loose, quiet as those boots of his, and speaks the word, and somewhere behind the wall a latch that no hand on this side could reach lets go with a soft click I feel more than hear. I put the rat in.

It is dark in there. Down—the hollow drops, a chute of dressed stone, and the rat goes with it and comes out somewhere below street level. No windows. The air is still and papery. I look through its eyes and let the whiskers do the rest: a desk. Papers on it, in stacks. Shelves. Supplies. A back office—the kind of room a house like this keeps for the work it doesn't do in front of customers.

And on the wall, where a person sitting at the desk would see it every time they looked up, a harp between the points of a crescent moon.

"Harp," Vukradin says, reading my face.

"Oh, wow."

"Is it—a storage room, or—"

"Desk. Papers. Supplies." I keep the rat still, whiskers up. "And that."

Nobody needs it explained. Everyone in the North knows the sign. This is the Harpers' room—a local headquarters, by the look of the desk and the weight of the paper on it—sitting in the basement of a high-end tavern in a low-end district, with a hole in its wall that pays a man named Denvar to bring one crate a night down into the dark.

"So the Harpers are smuggling," I say.

"Smuggling," says Valphine, "or arranging for the ninth crate. Or receiving it. Or paying for somebody else who wants it. It isn't the same thing."

"Clearly that crate involves smuggling. It's delivered by hand to a hole in a wall at three in the morning. So the Harpers are smuggling in *some* capacity." Whether it's their crate or their client's crate, I don't know yet. I am not going to pretend I do.

The Harpers are not good people. I have heard this from enough people I trust to hold it as more than rumor, and I hold it now, looking at that harp through a rat's eyes. "The only good people left are the Order of the Gauntlet," someone says, and I say, "Debatable," and Valphine says, "Are there?"

"Reformed," Vukradin insists. "They're reformed. They had that whole thing with the barbarians—"

"They were misled by their political masters."

"That's what I'm saying. They've rejected those masters. They're the *Reformed* Order of the Gauntlet. All about the little guy now."

"Right, right," I say. "Justice and clean living and a hand up for the small folk." Different comic book, as Vukradin would say. I have my own opinions about how long a reform lasts once someone offers it money.

I bring the rat back up the chute and out through the wall before anybody in that basement comes looking for the click, and Vukradin seats the stone.

Then we go in the front, because Brewbarry is in there and because we have said we'd do both.

The House of a Thousand Faces is not a Bluelake room. There are mirrors on the walls, tall ones, so that you see yourself coming in twice. There are mannequins in the corners wearing clothes nobody has worn in fifty years—high collars, stiff sleeves, a color of green that went out with somebody's grandmother. The couches are deep and lush and not sticky. The fire in the grate actually draws, which in this city is a kind of miracle all by itself; I know a chimney when I smell one, and this one works. It is a fine room. It is a room that costs money to keep. It is exactly wrong for the street it sits on, the way a good coat is wrong on a man who carries boxes.

Brewbarry is sitting by the fire with his halberd against the couch and the expression of a man who has finally come in out of the cold. He looks up when we enter and his face says he has already decided this is a good place. He is not wrong, in his way. The couches are good.

"It's a good place," I say, to no one, standing in the doorway with the rat back on my shoulder and the harp still in my eyes. "I need to leave it."

Nobody moves. We are all inside now. The fire draws, and somewhere under our feet there is a desk with a great deal of paper on it, and someone who pays a man one night at a time to bring one crate down.
