## Soma — Harpers Behind the Wall

The rat comes back the same way it went. A circle of chalk, a little of the stuff Meril left me, and the hour of quiet work my hands can do without the rest of me. I am tired enough that the tiredness has gone past my knees and into the shell. That is a particular tiredness. You feel it in the seams.

But the rat is whole again, sitting in my palm, cleaning its face like nothing happened. Rats are like that. I put it inside my collar where it is warm and go back to watching Denvar's door.

Vukradin finds us in the grey before dawn. He has been talking into the Sending Stone all night, or listening to us talk into it, so he arrives already knowing that Denvar went home, slept, and has not yet come out. He does not look tired. Bards, I think, run on something other than sleep.

"You've been giving me updates through the stone," he says. "So I should probably join you. It's been a few hours."

"Yeah, yeah. We can have you come along." I shift my weight off the bad hip. "I can tell you about Denvar."

"Why don't I tell you about the local wererat mafia?"

He tells us. A wererat named Rsolk, tunnels under the district, a spider infestation the wererats want cleared out. There are wererats all over this city, apparently. I say so, and that I expected them to be such big players, and Vukradin does not hear the dryness in it because he has already arrived at the part that matters to him.

"We just gotta clear out these spiders and it's more money for the studio. This is great. Finally, a business opportunity." He looks at each of us in turn so nobody misses it. "Fair Trade Gold."

Spider gold. I have pulled enough spiders out of enough nets to know the smell of it. I do not argue. The door across the street is opening.

---

Denvar walks like a man with somewhere to be. We follow at the distance of a street. He does not go far. He comes to a stretch of bare stone wall, the back of some building where it meets the alley, and there he does the thing careful men do when they think they are being subtle: he stops, and he looks both ways, slowly, and then he looks both ways again.

"It's a dead drop," Vukradin says under his breath.

Denvar puts his hand on a stone that looks like every other stone. It comes out. His hand goes in after it and comes back with a small bag. The stone goes back. He looks at what is in his hand — a chit, Valphine says later, she had her eyes on him — and he smiles, and he walks off down the alley smiling, and that is the whole of it.

"Payday," I say. "I'm going to the casino."

We let him go. Vukradin wants to, and for once I want the same thing. The man has been paid. Whatever he knows, he has already done with it. Barring coming back tomorrow before he leaves his house, and we do not know how early that is, Denvar might be a dead end. I say that too. Nobody disagrees.

"Eyeball the wall," I say.

"Valphine," Vukradin says, "do you want to do your—"

"Infinite perception," I offer.

"On him, or on the—"

"On the wall."

She looks at the stone the way she looks at everything, which is like it owes her money. The stone comes out for her the same as it did for Denvar. Behind it is a cavity, and behind the cavity is a second closure, and that one does not move. It is a two-way box. Somebody on the far side can put a thing in and somebody on this side can take it out, and there is no way, from here, to know which side the payment came from.

"Is it like a two-way window," Vukradin says, "where you put it in on one side and close the door, and then they—"

"We'd have to go inspect it," I say.

"Right. We'd have to go inspect it." He is looking at the wall the way he looks at a problem he would like to solve without walking into a room full of strangers. "I'm trying to think whether we have something that can help us without going into the house."

"We have Mold Earth," I say. "But I know you won't."

"It's not earth. It's stone."

"Yeah, it's gotta be a Margaster residence," I say. "Or something like that." I do not know that. It is the shape my suspicion takes when it has nothing to hold on to.

"It's not a residence," Valphine says. "It's not a shop."

"You don't think?"

"That's what I'm questioning. Is it a residence or a shop? Let's loop around."

So we loop around. It is a stone building, well-made, with a proper front on the next street over. There is a sign. There are lamps still lit in the windows though the sun is coming up. There is a smell of bread and something roasting that reaches all the way to where I am standing and does things to me I would rather it did not.

The House of a Thousand Faces. A tavern. Open.

"It is early in the morning," Valphine observes.

"Somebody's obviously in there paying off shady debts," I say. It is not a joke. A tavern that opens before the bakers is a tavern with a second business.

"Okay," Vukradin says. "Sure. We can hit the tavern. It's early, but why not."

"It's five o'clock somewhere."

Brewbarry has stopped hearing us. He is looking at the sign with an expression I have not seen on him before. It takes me a moment to place it. It is the look of a hatchling who has been told all season that the good tidepool is for the older ones and has just discovered that he is, now, one of the older ones.

"I know this place," he says. And he goes in. Just like that. The door swings and I get a glimpse of the inside — mirrors, a great deal of them, and figures standing along the wall that I take for people until I see they are mannequins, dressed in cuts nobody has worn since before I was hatched, and couches deep enough to lose a person in, and a fire that is actually drawing, which is more than most fires in this district can say — and then the door closes and he is gone into it.

"He was born in the tavern," I say.

"To be fair," Valphine says, "he has heard about this place for some time. When he first came to Neverwinter he could not go there. He did not have the money."

"Right," Vukradin says. "It's more of a high-end establishment. He was more of a Common Chord slum, and this was a high-end establishment."

"He's a man of class now," I say. "He can buy his way into high society."

"Isn't it obvious?" Valphine, flat as a slate. "It's the quality of your talent."

"Not," Vukradin says.

"It's totally not. You're sure."

"It definitely is not." And here his voice does the thing it does when he has a case to build. "There is a force behind our fame. Some groups are maneuvering. We're being—"

"Manipulated," I say.

"—to their advantage, for some reason."

That is true and we all know it is true. Somebody has been making Lathander's Death famous faster than a band that plays a tavern in Phandalin ought to be. Somebody has been paying for it. We do not know who, or why. Nobody says more than that. There is not more to say.

---

"I have an idea," Vukradin says, "for investigating without going inside. Knock."

"Sorry — how does that work? You use Knock to open the two-way dead drop?"

"Right. We can't physically open the other side, but I think we could open it magically. And then we could look in and see what room we're looking at. Or we could even send one of your rats in there to look around."

I feel the rat shift against my neck at the word *rat*. "I like that combo."

I do. I would have walked in the front door, myself. That is what I am for. I am not the one in this bale who thinks in spells; I think in doors and windows and which way the water runs. But he proposed it, and it is worth a shot.

"I have a different strategy," Valphine says. "We go in there and offer them a business proposal."

"Yeah," I say. "Vukradin could play."

"Your booking agency."

"Getting backstage would give you access to staff-only areas," I say, "where you could do your music preparation. And by music preparation—"

"There you go," Valphine says.

Vukradin has his hands up, one for each side of the scale. "We could cast two spells. Or we could have an—"

"Or we could get paid!"

"—an extended contract process, and several performance roles, and hours of time." His hands come down. He looks at the door of the tavern, where the lamps are still lit and the fire is drawing and somewhere inside a man he calls Homer is sitting on a couch he has wanted to sit on for years. "I do like to perform, though."

"Yeah, see, I mean." I sigh. "These are tough choices."

Nobody says anything.

"Let's do both. Why not both."

"Exactly," Vukradin says, relieved. "Why not."

So: back to the alley. Open our side, cast Knock on the other side, send the rat through, look around. Then walk in the front with the rest of it. We want to at least not walk in completely blind.

"It's a simple strategy," Valphine says, in the tone she uses for things she considers beneath simplicity.

"Yeah," I say.

---

The stone comes out. Vukradin puts his hand to the inner closure and speaks the word, and there is a sound — not loud, but a *sound*, the kind you hear in your teeth — and the closure gives. Beyond it is dark.

I take the rat out of my collar. It looks at me. I look at it. Then I set it in the cavity and close my eyes and let the tortle go.

Rat is small. Rat is close to the ground and the ground is everything. The stone is cold under four feet and smells of the alley — piss, rain, the ghost of a cat that came through here some days ago. Then the box, and then the drop on the far side, a short one, onto a floor.

Wood. Swept, but not recently. The air is still and does not move the whiskers, which means no window, no draught, a closed room. It is dark to the tortle but not to the rat. Shapes: legs of a desk, thick and turned. The underside of a chair. A basket with papers curling over the edge of it. Shelves up the wall with the smell of ink and sealing wax and lamp oil and the flat dry smell of paper that has been stacked a long time.

I take the rat up the leg of the desk. Papers. A lot of them. I cannot read them through the rat and I do not try. Supplies — quills, a knife, a stick of wax worn to the length of a thumb. Somebody works here. Somebody sits at this desk in a room with no windows and closes the far side of a box in the wall.

And on the wall, above the desk, where a person sitting would look up and see it, a harp between the horns of a silver moon.

I know that symbol. Everybody who has lived in the North a while knows that symbol, whether or not they have reason to. I have never had reason to.

I open my eyes. The alley is very bright after the rat's dark and the cold comes back into my hip all at once.

"Harp," I say.

"Harp?" Vukradin says. "Like — a *harp?*"

"Oh, wow," I say. I do not usually say things like that.

"Okay, so — is it, like, a storage room, or—"

"A back office. No windows. Desk. Papers. Supplies." I am watching the tavern door over his shoulder. "Basement level. The rat went down."

"You wouldn't steal anything," Valphine says, to nobody in particular.

"No," Vukradin says. "Definitely wouldn't steal anything."

"They're right," I say. "I can't carry anything." The rat is halfway back through the box already. I can feel it coming.

"So it's — headquarters," Valphine says. "For the local Harpers."

"Harpers." I try the word out. "So that's — weird. The Harpers are smuggling?"

"Well," Vukradin says slowly, and I can hear him laying it out for himself, "are the Harpers smuggling, or are the Harpers arranging for that ninth crate?"

"Clearly that ninth crate involves some smuggling, because it's being directly delivered. So yes. The Harpers are smuggling in some capacity." I say it before he can finish softening it. Whether they packed the crate, or are waiting for the crate, or are running the whole thing for somebody who pays them the way somebody paid Denvar — that we do not know. That the harp is on the wall behind the box: that we know.

They are not good people, the Harpers of this city. Whatever they are in the songs. I have heard enough from enough people to know the songs are old.

"The only good people," Vukradin says, "are the Order of the Gauntlet."

"Debatable," I say.

"Are there?" Valphine says.

"They're reformed," Vukradin insists. "They had that whole thing with the barbarians, you know. They were misled by their political masters, and they've rejected those masters now. They recognized they were being used by powerful forces. Now they're all about the little guy. The Reformed Order of the Gauntlet."

"Truth and justice," I say, "and the little guy."

He does not hear the edge. That is fine. The edge is for me.

The rat climbs back into my hand, cold-footed, and I put it away against my neck where it can warm up.

Then I look at the House of a Thousand Faces again, and it is a different building than it was. The lamps burning at dawn. The fire that draws so well, in a district where nothing draws well. The mirrors, so a person sitting anywhere in that room can see every door. The mannequins in their old clothes, standing along the wall like the room has always had people in it who do not move and do not talk and do not leave. A high-end place in a low-end district, and I had thought that was somebody's vanity. It is not vanity. It is a good place. It has been a good place for a long time, on purpose, for a reason, and the reason has an office under it with no windows.

And Brewbarry is inside it, sitting on one of those couches with his money, because he earned it, because he wanted to, because it is the first time in his life the door opened for him and he did not think to ask who opened it.

I have him in my bale. I have all of them. I am standing in an alley with a rat in my collar and Meril's staff in my hand, and the staff is no heavier than it was an hour ago, and I could stand here another hour telling myself that.

"Yep," I say. "It's a good place. I need to leave it."

Nobody moves yet. The front door is around the corner, and the lamps are still lit.
