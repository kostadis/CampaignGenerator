# Chapter 51: We’re famous. Why are we famous?

## Vukradin — Patrons of the Chord

“The Chord’s unaffordable now,” the man says. “And it’s all because of Vukradin.”

I am close enough to hear him without trying. Close enough, in fact, that I could thank him for recognizing my contribution, if the contribution he means were not the exclusion of people who want to hear music.

Around us, the Common Chord shines. Brewbarry’s table has leather on it now, and a plaque, and the food arriving at nearby tables requires rather more explanation than food used to require here. This is the place that once threw me out unpaid. Now it is the hardest ticket in Neverwinter.

There ought to be a happy interval between those two conditions in which a musician gets paid and an ordinary person gets to listen.

I turn toward the man.

“Actually, I’m planning to open an independent music studio in Phandalin. Low-cost, nonprofit—a way to hear Lathander’s Death regularly, without all of this becoming an obstacle. If you’d like to contribute to the charitable fund, that would help us open sooner.”

He looks from my face to the room, then back again.

“And spread the word,” I add. “That matters too.”

Behind me, our preparations for the night continue in fits. Aldus still has not confirmed the time of our dinner with Lord Neverember. Until somebody supplies an hour, the lord can wait.

“Neverember or Neverwinter?” I asked earlier. “I’m kidding.”

“He’s never flaming,” Soma said. “He keeps it quiet.”

Lord Neverflaming. Much easier to remember, although perhaps not something to say at dinner.

The rumor is that he has a new drow advisor. Valphine thinks it could be one of her siblings, and I suspect the same, but we have a rumor and a dinner invitation, not a family reunion.

“Everyone’s related to everyone,” Soma observed.

There is also Brewbarry’s bathrobe business to consider. The capital is secured. The cost of imports remains ridiculous because the shipping routes and portals are still disrupted, and House Margaster controls the available logistics.

Of course they do.

They also deliver on time and on schedule, which I acknowledge because fairness does not become optional when somebody irritates me.

Valphine regards them as competition.

She says it calmly enough that I glance at her to determine whether she means commercially. Her expression offers no useful clarification.

The man in front of me fumbles for his money.

“I haven’t got much,” he says.

Then he puts ten gold pieces into my hand.

For a moment I simply look at them. Ten gold. From somebody whose complaint was that he could no longer afford this place.

“That’s very generous. Thank you. Truly.”

“That’s a hell of a donation,” Soma says.

“This goes in the studio fund,” I tell her. “Separate from the regular coffers.”

The donor pauses before withdrawing his hand entirely.

“Do I get on something? Some kind of signage? For people who contributed?”

“Absolutely. Program credit.”

“For how long?”

“A year. If you want to be in next year’s program, you’d donate again, but a year is a long time.”

“And ten gold gets me—?”

“There are different giving levels.”

“Premier or Plus?” Soma supplies.

“Plus,” I say. “You’re at Plus. Not quite Premier yet.”

He considers this, then nods with the pleased gravity of a man who has discovered he occupies a named position in the arts.

We record his contribution. A studio needs walls and equipment and somewhere for people to sit, certainly, but it also needs people who want it to exist. I have ten gold pieces in my hand and one such person standing in front of me.

Good.

There is time before we need to reach Lim’s deliveries at three in the morning. I approach five more patrons.

The first recognizes me immediately.

“The real Vukradin?”

“Yes.”

“Biggest fan. Biggest fan. But I’ve already done my charitable giving for the year.”

“That’s fine. I’m not here to pressure anybody. I just like fans.” I glance toward Brewbarry. “Bigger than Brewbarry, though?”

“Brewbarry is very large,” Valphine says. “Occasionally huge.”

“Nobody’s as big as Brewbarry. That’s just how it is.”

The man relaxes. We can have a pleasant conversation without money changing hands. This should not be a difficult principle.

Wick has almost the opposite difficulty. He does not know Lathander’s Death, doubts that I am Vukradin, and is delighted to support my music school.

“Studio,” Soma says.

“School, studio—if you have a studio, people can train there,” I say. “That’s part of what it’s for.”

Wick nods enthusiastically. My identity is apparently the least convincing part of the proposal, but the proposal itself has found a friend.

Sabbatha listens while looking past me.

“He’s very committed to the look,” she says.

I follow her gaze to Brewbarry.

“Yes. He is quite bulging, isn’t he?”

“Rippling,” Soma adds.

“Exactly.”

Brewbarry stands where he has been standing, unaware, or perhaps simply unconcerned, that his appearance has become part of the fundraising presentation.

By the time I reach Old Hesp, I have names to preserve: Wick, Sabbatha, Ondrelle Kass. Hesp supplies his own before I can ask.

“I remember when you used to play to an empty room.”

“Did you come? Did I give you your money back?”

“You said if I didn’t give it ten out of ten, you wouldn’t take my money.”

“Yes. Well, what do you think now?”

He looks around the Chord. The pause lasts long enough for somebody to pass with a plate of fancy food.

“Now this is a friggin’ expensive place to be. Everybody’s got to look cool.”

“Well, come to the studio when it’s up and running.”

“It doesn’t have the feel anymore. That gritty—”

“We’re going to get it back.”

“Too commercial. Petra’s turned it into a money-making operation. I’ve got to respect her for it, but that table—”

He points at Brewbarry’s table.

“Wasn’t leather. Wasn’t shiny. Didn’t have a plaque.”

I look at the plaque. It commemorates something he remembers without needing a plaque at all.

“And we didn’t have this kind of food.”

“We’re going to get it back,” I tell him again, more quietly.

I write down Old Hesp’s name.

Between conversations, we learn something less encouraging than patronage. Talk at the bar turns to the sewers, and to the tide that comes through them from the river.

“There’s a tide in the sewers?” I ask. “The river has a tide?”

Soma gives me a look appropriate to somebody who has spent rather more time near the sea.

Neverwinter is beside the coast. Yes. Fine. The sea can evidently pursue us underground.

We establish that Selûne is the moon involved, that the Tears of Selûne are not another moon, and that I have briefly made the timing more complicated than it needs to be.

“Two high tides and two low tides,” I say, settling it. “About six hours from high to low. We want to be there at low tide.”

“Three in the morning,” Soma says.

“So we have three useful hours, maybe four, before things become really uncomfortable.”

Soma appears less troubled by the water than by the prospect of waiting for us to understand it.

By departure, the studio fund is twenty gold richer. Petra’s five hundred for my likeness remains pending until the performance two days from now. I do not count it as money in hand.

Twenty gold, freely given. Names for a program that does not exist yet. People who want to hear us.

I put the money away carefully.

“All right,” I say. “Let’s go do some spelunking.”

## Vukradin — Nine Crates at Low Tide

The steps beside the sewer grille are wet enough to return a little light. They descend in the orderly fashion of an aqueduct, which lends the whole place an air of public usefulness entirely at odds with how it smells.

Soma casts *Find Familiar*. A rat appears, small and ready for work.

“I can see and hear through it,” she says. “Within a hundred feet.”

“I can handle anything that needs to be done stealthily.”

“I didn’t know bards were especially quiet.”

“Boots of Elvenkind.”

“Hell yeah.”

I place one foot on the stone without a sound. There are many reasons to appreciate elven craftsmanship. At present, the most important is that it allows me to be excellent at something nobody associates with talking.

The carriers arrive with nine crates.

Eight go into a stack for Lim. None of those eight bears the Commission Harbor mark. We already suspected the goods were arriving outside the ordinary channels; seeing the unmarked boxes does nothing to improve the situation.

The ninth crate goes onto a separate ledge.

The man who places it there pauses. He looks down into the dark beneath the grille, as if checking whether somebody is ready, then leaves.

His companion finishes stacking. They walk off in different directions without looking at each other.

“That nonchalant whistling,” Soma murmurs.

Neither needs actually to whistle. The performance is complete without it.

“We have two things to follow,” I whisper. “The ninth crate, and the people who dropped it. If the carriers split, you’ll have to choose.”

Valphine watches the man who checked the darkness.

“That one. The other was stacking boxes. This one appears to know what happens next.”

“I’d follow him,” Soma agrees. “He knows something’s coming out.”

We make the division. Valphine, Soma, and Brewbarry will trail the knowledgeable carrier. I will wait for the ninth crate’s collector.

This is a sensible arrangement built from our individual strengths. It is also an arrangement in which I may enter the sewers alone.

“That seems pretty dangerous,” I admit.

Soma looks at me.

Then something emerges beneath the grille.

It is not the person we expected. It is cat-sized, brass and leather, quick without seeming hurried. It goes directly to the separate crate. No searching, no uncertainty, no inspection of the eight others. It takes the ninth and turns back below with the competence of a thing following instructions.

There it is. Our lead.

“I’ll follow.”

“This is my concern,” Soma says. “You end up going into the sewers now. One person.”

“I’ll be fine.”

“Tell me the odds.”

“Only a one-in-four-hundred chance of disaster. It’ll be fine.”

That estimate concerns the likelihood of my making a truly catastrophic noise. It seems persuasive enough to cover the broader question.

I touch the Sending Stone.

“You have the other one. We can keep in touch.”

“Good to know.”

“And remember, we promised Lim we wouldn’t spook her connections.”

Soma gives me a remarkably level stare for somebody crouched beside a sewer.

“Thank you so much.”

Then they are following the man, and I am following the construct.

The smell thickens below. Damp catches at my face, and the stone underfoot changes from wet to slick in patches that demand attention. My boots remain silent. Ahead, brass moves through the dark with a crate that matters very much to somebody.

I keep my distance.

There is a useful satisfaction in doing a difficult thing well. Nobody here needs persuading. Nobody requires a charitable tier. I need only follow, quietly, and discover where the crate goes.

The tunnel continues.

And suddenly I cannot imagine why I am wasting my time with it.

The feeling is complete. Not a question to work through. Not a cautious reconsideration. This route is dull, pointless, beneath the smallest expenditure of further effort.

I remember the ninth crate. I remember the ledge, the brass collector, Soma’s concern. None of that has gone anywhere. I simply find that this particular pursuit no longer deserves my attention.

The construct keeps going.

I turn away.

There must be a more useful direction than that one. I choose another passage with the immediate relief of somebody finally abandoning a tedious conversation.

My feet make no sound at all.

## Vukradin — Lost in Rsolk’s Tunnels

“How the hell did you get over here?”

A man stands in front of several others. They occupy enough of the tunnel that going past them would require an understanding we have not yet reached.

“I don’t know,” I say. “Where am I?”

He looks at me.

I look back. His expression suggests that my answer has failed to address the essential issue.

“Because I’m not over there,” I explain.

The water makes small noises against the stone. Nobody laughs.

I do know why I entered the sewers. I followed a crate. I left its route because—because following it seemed pointless, which is becoming a less satisfactory explanation now that I have to consider it in front of strangers.

As for my present location, ignorance is something I can offer with confidence.

“I’m lost. Wrong turn.”

The man moistens a finger against his tongue.

“This is my territory.”

“Your territory. Okay. Did you want me to go?”

“I want to know why you’re in my territory.”

We are close to achieving a circle.

“I’m sorry I got lost. I’m happy to leave. Could you point me toward Lim’s? The café. That’s where I want to be headed.”

“Lim’s Café?”

“Yes. We’re friends.”

His attention sharpens.

That is not the response I expect to the mention of a friend. I cannot tell what conclusion he is approaching, only that he seems to be examining me more closely on the way there.

Then he tilts his head.

“Are you that singer?”

“Exactly. And Lim’s a fan.”

“What are you doing down here, man?”

“I told you. I got lost!”

“Vukradin. You’re Vukradin, right?”

One of the men beside him breaks into a smile.

“Is this really Vukradin?”

There we are. Common ground.

“Do you guys want autographs?”

They do. Very much.

“Sure. We can do autographs. But then I have to be going, so if you could point me in the right direction—”

“We’ll do one better,” the leader says. “We’ll take you there.”

“All right. Sounds good.”

“Don’t want you getting lost again, Mr. Vukradin.”

“Exactly. Lim will be thrilled.”

His name is Rsolk. Now that we have established who I am and that my presence is a navigational failure rather than an incursion, he proves willing to explain the territory.

“It’s dangerous down here,” he says.

“Is it? In your territory? I’d have thought you’d keep it very safe. You seem like a safe individual. I’m sure you keep the place shipshape.”

“Mr. Vukradin, I agree with you.”

This is encouraging.

“But I have to tell you why I was concerned when I saw you. I’m glad I recognized you. One of my guys says you’ve got a deal with a friend of mine.”

“At the Common Chord?”

“No. At the Toe mine. Zeleen.”

“Mountain’s Toe Mine. Oh! I didn’t know you knew Zeleen.”

“Said there was a business relationship with you. I said that was full of shit.”

“No, that’s true. We spent some time in Phandalin.” I pause. “They haven’t produced very much yet, but they say they’re working hard.”

Rsolk laughs.

“That’s how I feel about it too.”

He laughs again, and the men behind him settle into an easier arrangement. I am still in an unfamiliar tunnel, but I have ceased to be an unexplained object blocking its operation.

“Look,” he says, “I manage the tunnels here.”

“Oh, you’re the tunnel manager.”

“Yeah. A tunnel manager.”

“So you have all the tunnels?”

“Not all the tunnels. The tunnels in this area.”

“Right. Important distinction.”

He describes a passage near the floodline, one that floods regularly. I try to attach his directions to the turns I have taken. This would be considerably easier if I had taken them for reasons.

“About two months ago, things started coming up there.”

“Creature things?”

“We’ve lost men. We don’t go there anymore.”

That changes the conversation.

The discomfort of being challenged in somebody else’s territory recedes. These men know the passages; they work in them, depend on them, and have reached a place they will no longer enter because people who went there did not return.

“Do you need help with that? My friends and I have some useful abilities, and we’re looking for ways to make money. Maybe we can help you.”

Rsolk needs little convincing. He moves at once to the account of a man who escaped.

“Something he’d never seen before. Looked like a spider, but the colors were all wrong.”

I stop trying to reconstruct my turns.

“The webs too,” he says. “Something wrong with them. You could see it.”

I remember webbing that did not look as webbing should. The unpleasantness of it returns before any useful name does.

“We have experience with that.”

Useful experience. Hard-earned experience. The sort I would prefer not to have acquired, but which ought to benefit somebody now that we possess it.

I expect Rsolk to discuss a reward. Instead, his manner becomes almost apologetic.

“Mr. Vukradin, I know this is too much to ask, but could you get me some tickets to the Common Chord?”

Even here.

I can follow a mysterious construct into a sewer, wander into somebody’s territory, and discover that the obstacle in need of negotiation is still admission to the Common Chord.

“I can ask,” I say. “We have kind of an arm’s-length relationship with the venue. But I will ask for you.”

“That’s all I could ask for.”

I do not promise tickets I cannot supply. I do promise to ask.

Then I sign the autographs.

It takes a little care to find a position where damp hands and damp surroundings will not make a mess of the writing. The smiling man watches my hand with the same attention Rsolk initially gave my face. I make the signature clear.

They give me what I need to find Rsolk again and show me the way toward familiar ground. I listen carefully this time.

There is work here for my friends. People need help, and we may be able to provide it.

First, however, I need to get out of this sewer.

## Soma — Denvar’s Unsettled Account

The carrier eats alone.

It is the fourth hour. Only a few cook shops are open, and he has chosen one of them. Not Lim’s. Steam rises from his food. He pays attention to it, which is the first sensible thing I have seen anyone do in hours.

“Dinner,” I murmur.

“Breakfast,” Valphine says.

“Night shift.”

The distinction does not improve our information. He meets nobody. He delivers no whispered message. He sits and eats while my knees stiffen.

“He appears to be a delivery man,” Valphine says. “I see no reason to promote him to mastermind.”

“Somebody should tell his wages.”

We leave him to his meal. Lim has a business to keep alive. I have no intention of frightening her connections merely because one of them stops for food.

When he finishes, we follow him into the Bluelake District.

His lodging house packs many rooms into a small amount of building. High efficiency. More doors than privacy, I expect, though our man reaches his room and goes to sleep without troubling anyone.

I send in the rat.

Through its eyes, furniture becomes an arrangement of legs and overhead surfaces. I guide it around the room while the sleeper breathes steadily. No factional insignia. No useful personal marks. At first, no receipts or ordinary records of pay.

His belongings are better than I expect. The clothes, the small comforts, the meal bought at four in the morning. None proves a crime. Together they suggest that carrying these particular boxes pays well.

“He’s a little overcompensated,” I tell the others.

Brewbarry waits with his halberd. Waiting comes more easily to him when there is a person to watch than when there is a conversation to understand.

“Found himself a good gig,” I add. “Oh shit. I want that gig.”

We still need a name.

Valphine looks over the building.

“He has a landlord. We could ask questions.”

By morning, we have one available.

“What does it cost to live here?” I ask.

The landlord looks at my shell.

“Don’t see many turtles around here.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

“I thought turtles carried their house on their shoulders. Didn’t need a place to stay.”

I shift Meril’s staff in my hand. My shell has been carrying its own weight through the whole night. I would welcome the addition of a hearth and a chair, if anatomy worked that way.

“I can’t live in this thing. It merely protects me. I have a cabin outside Neverwinter.”

“Oh. Learned a new thing. Didn’t know turtles didn’t live in their shells. I suppose you aren’t like turtles.”

“That’s getting a little slanderous.”

His eyes move past me to Valphine and Brewbarry. He takes in rather more of them this time.

“I’m just asking questions.”

“Oh. You’re a just-asking-questions man.”

“Just asking questions.”

“I’ll pretend I didn’t hear some of that. What does it cost, and what sort of people stay here? Say I’m looking for a place for my shell sprouts.”

“First of all, we don’t care. Equal-opportunity establishment.”

“So progressive.”

“Money’s money, man. I don’t care who’s got it.”

Then he turns to Valphine.

“You’re a drow.”

“Very astute.”

“Thought drow didn’t like sunlight.”

“Oh, I love the sunlight.”

She says it without helping him understand. I leave her to it. Educating this man appears to be an occupation with long hours.

He finally explains the terms. Significant cash up front. Enough to make our sleeper upstairs more interesting. This is not where an ordinary day laborer stretches the last of his wages.

“You don’t check anything?” I ask. “Whether they’ll have the gold they claim they’ll have?”

“I don’t scry on my customers.”

“I’m sure you’re more curious about some than others.”

He looks at Brewbarry’s chest. Then at the halberd.

“Look, I don’t want trouble. If you want a room, I’ll give you a discount.”

“We really want to know about one of your tenants.”

I describe the man we followed.

“Denvar?”

A name at last. All it took was a housing inquiry and a survey of the landlord’s opinions on ancestry.

“Yes. Denvar. He dropped something while he was skulking about late last night. A little pouch. We’d like to return it.”

“Making a delivery,” Valphine adds.

“Yes. Making a delivery.”

The landlord accepts the explanation well enough to talk. Denvar is quiet. Reliable. Doesn’t bother anybody.

Upstairs, my rat’s continued search produces something better than the landlord’s endorsement.

Delivery chits.

I let the familiar’s view fill my attention again. The papers carry a client reference and a house mark. No personal name. The same instruction repeats:

*9 crates, 3rd hour, 8 up, 1 down.*

I examine the next. Then the next.

“All of them,” I tell Valphine. “Exactly the same. He literally has one job.”

About a tenday’s records, seven chits in the sequence. Weekly settlement. Payment stamps reconcile the earlier ones.

The seventh has no stamp.

The newest. The delivery we watched.

I return to my own eyes. My body is where I left it, stiff and in need of breakfast.

“Is Denvar settled up on his bills?” I ask the landlord.

“Owes me some money today.”

There it is.

“He’s asleep right now, you know.”

“He’s got until the end of the day. Good customer. Quiet. Doesn’t bother anybody.”

“I’ll bet.”

“Good man, then?” Valphine asks.

“Yeah. Totally.”

“You don’t think he’s a serial killer or anything?” I ask.

The landlord looks at my staff. At Valphine’s white armor and glowing eyes. At Brewbarry’s halberd. He inventories the visible threats while considering the invisible one upstairs.

“Should I be worried?”

“You never know with these types.”

“I would be worried about him,” Valphine says.

We have enough. Denvar needs money today. The latest delivery has not been settled. Once he wakes, he has a reason to find whoever pays him.

We need to keep watching.

As we turn away, the landlord calls after me.

“Soma, right?”

I stop.

“Now I know turtles are looking for housing, what makes a good turtle room?”

I could make this expensive for him. A very particular ceiling. Imported sand. Some elaborate nonsense he would repeat to the next tortle unfortunate enough to ask his prices.

“A nice hearth,” I say. “Plenty of water. Near a body of water is good.”

He listens this time.

I leave it there. Somewhere, eventually, someone may get a decent room out of the conversation.

## Soma — Harpers Behind the Wall

The wait takes several more hours.

I cast *Find Familiar* again to restore the rat. A small body for narrow spaces. Mine has spent enough time standing outside a lodging house.

Vukradin returns with his boots quiet and his mouth making up the difference.

The Sending Stone updates have brought him to us. I tell him what matters about Denvar: the repeated chits, the unpaid seventh, the rent due today.

“Why don’t I tell you about the local wererat mafia?” he says.

“There are wererats all over, aren’t there?”

He explains Rsolk, the tunnels, the recognition, the autographs. He also explains that something made him lose interest in following the construct.

I look him over. He is intact. This permits me to listen to the business opportunity.

“We just have to clear out these spiders, and it’s more money for the studio,” he says. “Finally a business opportunity. Fair Trade Gold.”

Wrong-colored spiders. Wrong webs. Men lost near the floodline. He has directions to find Rsolk again.

My bale can turn a lost bard into a job offer. I would prefer fewer missing bards in the process.

Denvar wakes. We follow.

He takes us to a stone wall adjoining a building. He slows, checks his surroundings, and approaches a particular stone.

“Dead drop,” Vukradin murmurs.

Denvar opens it. His hand goes in and comes back with a bag. He closes the hiding place, looks at his chit, and smiles.

“Payday,” I say under my breath. “Off to the gambling tables.”

We let him go. His rent is a more likely destination, but I have watched him eat and sleep for most of the morning. I am entitled to a little speculation.

“Denvar may be a dead end now,” I say. “Unless we wait outside his house again before the next delivery.”

The wall is closer and does not require another night shift.

Valphine examines the stone. The compartment works in both directions. Someone could have placed the payment from the street or from inside. We cannot tell which by looking at this side.

“Let’s go around,” she says.

“Could we do something without entering?” Vukradin asks.

“I have *Mold Earth*.”

“It’s stone.”

“Yes. That does get in the way.”

I suspect a Margaster property. Stone, secret payments, expensive logistics. A plausible combination. The walk around the perimeter disproves at least the part where I imagine a private residence.

It is a tavern.

The House of a Thousand Faces.

Open already.

“Somebody’s in there paying shady debts,” I say.

Brewbarry looks at the entrance.

“I’ve heard of this place. Couldn’t afford it before.”

Then he walks in.

No committee. No discussion of cover identities. A man who can now afford a tavern enters a tavern.

“He’s a man of class now,” I say.

Vukradin peers toward the doorway. “More high-end than the old Common Chord.”

Through the open entrance I catch mirrors and deep couches. Mannequins wear clothes that were fashionable long enough ago to become fashionable again at an extra charge. The fire draws properly. No smoke hanging at head height. Somebody has paid attention to the chimney.

The place sits oddly among its neighbors. Too comfortable. Too carefully furnished for this part of Bluelake.

Vukradin turns back to us.

“I have an idea. *Knock*. We open our side of the compartment and cast it on the inner closure.”

“How does that work?”

“We can’t physically open the other side. We may be able to open it magically. Then your rat goes through and sees what room is there.”

That I understand.

“I like that combination.”

“I have a different strategy,” Valphine says. “We enter and offer a business proposal. Your booking agency provides a sufficient reason.”

“Vukradin could play,” I say. “Music preparation gets him into staff-only areas. And by music preparation—”

“There you go,” Valphine says.

Vukradin looks between us.

“We could cast two spells, or we could have an extended contract process, several performances, and hours of—”

“Or we get paid.”

He pauses.

“I do like to perform.”

“Tough choices. Let’s do both.”

The proposal remains a proposal. We still have a compartment to open, and Brewbarry has already supplied the party’s attendance inside.

Before we return to the wall, Valphine gestures toward the tavern.

“Isn’t your welcome into high society obviously the quality of your talent?”

“No,” Vukradin says.

“No? You’re certain?”

“It definitely is not.”

That gets my attention. He likes his talent. So do I, when it is not holding up a departure.

“We’re famous,” he says. “Why are we famous? There’s a force behind it. Some groups are maneuvering. We’re being—”

“Manipulated.”

“To their advantage, for some reason.”

He looks back at the House of a Thousand Faces. I think of the plaque at the Chord. The prices. The man who donated ten gold to recover something fame had priced out of his reach.

We know someone is amplifying Lathander’s Death. We do not know who. We do not know what they expect to collect.

For now, the wall has a smaller question we can answer.

We open the street side. Vukradin casts *Knock* on the inaccessible inner closure. The spell releases it, and the rat slips through.

I lean on Meril’s staff and surrender my sight.

Close stone passes around a small body. Then the space opens. The route leads down, below the tavern’s main floor.

A basement office. No windows.

I guide the rat along the edges. Desk. Papers. Supplies. Ordinary things at an inconvenient scale. I search for a mark that can tell us whose ordinary things they are.

And find it.

The Harper symbol.

Unmistakable.

For a moment I keep looking. Long enough to be certain, though certainty arrives at once.

“Oh,” I say. “Wow.”

“What?” Vukradin asks.

“Harpers.”

“Harp?”

“The Harpers. This looks like their local headquarters.”

The words bring the others into what I am seeing. Until I say them, it is only a rat in a room and me standing beside a wall.

“What sort of room?” Vukradin asks. “Storage?”

“Back office. Desk, papers, supplies. Basement level.”

I bring my attention back to my own body. The wall is in front of me again. Valphine waits beside it. Brewbarry is still inside the tavern.

“The Harpers are smuggling?” I say.

“At least this is their end of the payment arrangement,” Valphine says.

“The ninth crate is being delivered secretly. They’re involved in some capacity.”

But there is space between involvement and an answer. They could be arranging the ninth crate. Receiving it. Paying on behalf of someone else. The symbol does not tell us which, and the office has not explained the brass thing that carried the box away.

It certainly gives us no reason to trust the arrangement. The Harpers we know are not good people.

“The Order of the Gauntlet has at least reformed,” Vukradin says.

“Debatable.”

“They were misled by their political masters. The business with the barbarians—they’ve rejected those masters now.”

Valphine’s expression does not improve at the mention of political masters.

“Reformed Order of the Gauntlet,” Vukradin says. “They realized powerful people were using them.”

There is conviction in his voice. Also the fresh irritation of a man who has just named the thing he suspects is being done to him.

I look toward the tavern entrance.

Beyond it, mirrors make more room than the building owns. Old fashions stand empty on mannequins. The couches look soft enough to punish anyone who has been awake all night. Brewbarry is in there somewhere, enjoying the fact that he can afford to be.

We remain outside with a hidden office beneath us, an unexplained payment behind us, and directions to spider-infested tunnels for later.

It is a very nice tavern.

I would like my bale out of it.
