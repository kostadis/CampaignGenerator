Brewbarry has decided how this negotiation will be conducted, and the banker has not yet been informed.

"You're not gonna help me," he tells Aurelan, leaning over the desk so that the man's lamp is eclipsed. "I'm gonna help you, old man. Don't get things confused in that little head of yours. Now, please tell me, is there some business to be done here, or should I go elsewhere?"

I stand a little apart and observe. It is a technique I recognize, if not one I would have chosen. In House Sotorra a threat this open would have been considered vulgar, which is not the same as saying it did not work. What interests me is the banker. He does not flinch. He recalculates. A man who has sat behind this desk long enough to acquire that particular stillness has been shouted at before, and has learned that the shouting is a cost of doing business rather than a reason to stop doing it.

"Well," Aurelan says, "what are you making?"

"What do you mean, what am I baking?"

The banker does not correct him. "What the letter says is that Linene wishes us to lend her money so that she can start her business. She does not describe the nature of the business."

"Am I supposed to tell you what I'm making? I've got this letter here." Brewbarry taps it. When it becomes clear the letter will not speak for itself, he gathers himself. "I'm gonna show the world how cruel this world is, and I'm giving this softness to all the good people of the Forgotten Realms by supplying them with bathrobes."

It is, I note, a complete theology. Cruelty acknowledged; softness distributed; a garment as sacrament. My own temple runs on a not dissimilar logic in the opposite direction, and I have found that surface dwellers require the doctrine to be stated in exactly this register — sincere, slightly absurd, delivered by someone too large to laugh at — before they will fund it.

"That's very interesting." Aurelan draws a scrap of paper toward him and begins writing with a quill. "Do you know how much this will cost to make?"

"I have absolutely no idea, which is why I have a wealth management consultant. I suggest that you talk to her."

"Very well. I will make a note of talking to her. And how do you intend to ship your goods?"

"What do I look like? Some merchant? The logistics guy? No." Brewbarry straightens to his full height, which is considerable. "I hack and slash."

The banker writes this down as well. I would give a great deal to read that page.

He reads the letter again, and I watch the moment his face changes from a clerk's face to a banker's. "Oh. I see you intend to use Lionshield Coster as your shipping agent and business arrangement." He pauses. "Mr. Brewbarry, I wish to apologize. I want you to know I have no objection to Lionshield Coster's business — I have done wonderful business with them — but their rates have gone ridiculously up, and I'm afraid that, based on my calculations—"

He is fast. I have known accountants in Menzoberranzan who would take a full bell to reach a figure this man arrives at between one breath and the next, and they had the advantage of being able to kill anyone who disputed it. He reaches his conclusion and begins, with visible reluctance, to explain it.

"I will have to double-check the numbers, but I must be honest with you. With the margins we expect, even with Margaster's very generous margins and prices, and the cost of moving goods in and out of Phandalin, plus—" He is building toward a refusal. I can see the architecture of it; he is laying the foundation before the wall. "I don't think — I feel that the Counting House—"

"Sorry, let me, let me, let me cut you off." Brewbarry does not raise his voice, which is more effective than if he had. "Yadda yadda yadda yadda, you get my blood pumping. Just tell me you're gonna get it done, and that's it. Otherwise, I'm gonna get upset. You don't want to see me upset."

The banker's difficulty is arithmetic, and Brewbarry's answer to arithmetic is a promise of violence. It will get him the loan. It will not fix the sum. And the sum, as it happens, is wrong — not because the man cannot count, but because he is counting the wrong things.

"We're not tallying in two additional factors," I say.

Both of them look at me. Aurelan's quill stops.

"The temple markup." I let that settle. "And the church of Lathander's bulk demand."

I have watched this expression form on a great many faces. First the ordinary surprise of a man who did not expect the quiet one to speak. Then the second look, the longer one, in which he takes in the sun-sigil and the skin beneath it and arrives, with a small visible jolt, at the thing his eyes had been declining to report.

"Oh," he says. "You are one of the blessed of Lathander."

He pauses.

"And you're a drow."

"You're very perceptive, Aurelan," says Brewbarry.

I say nothing. There is nothing to say; the man is correct on both counts, and I have never understood why the surface finds the pairing so difficult to hold in one hand. Lathander's power is inexhaustible and unconcealed, and Lolth's is rationed and hidden. One does not need to be a banker to know which ledger to keep one's money in. But I file the reaction. A man who is startled by me will underestimate me for exactly as long as it takes him to recover, and I have made use of shorter intervals than that.

He recovers by retreating to the one thing he is sure of. "But Mr. Brewbarry, I wish to inform you that I do have a solution to your problem. Since your business is in production, what I would suggest is that instead of using Lionshield Coster for distribution, you reach out to House Margaster. They are the ones who have managed to reduce the pricing of goods quite significantly, and I believe with their margins and their arrangements you would be able to run the business most efficiently. If you could secure a letter of agreement with them — a letter of agreement — we would be more than delighted to sign on the dotted line."

There it is. I had been waiting to see where the referral would fall. A banker who will not lend on the terms in front of him but will lend on terms that route through a third party is not describing a risk; he is describing a relationship. Whether he is paid for it or merely comfortable in it hardly matters. The shape is the same.

Brewbarry's read of it is less structural and arrives faster.

"Do I look like a fool?" He unslings the halberd — not a swing, not a threat in any form a magistrate could name — and lays the haft across his other palm, and taps it there. Once. Twice. "Do I look like a fool, Aurelan?"

The tapping continues at the tempo of a slow heartbeat.

"Exactly. And you tell me to go and do some business with somebody else. You think I'm an idiot? Did you have, like, some sweet deal with this other guy? I didn't give two shit about it. Now, I told you, I have a wealth management consultant, she has a supply company, she's gonna borrow money from you, you're gonna lend it to her, and you're gonna get this done."

It is not subtle. It is also not wrong. The halberd is doing what the calculations did for the banker: it is a visible object that makes the abstract thing — the refusal, the threat — impossible to pretend one has not seen.

Vukradin steps in at the exact moment the tapping has done its work and would, continued, begin to undo it. This is the thing I admire in him and cannot forgive: the timing is perfect, and he appears to believe he is simply being reasonable.

"Surely," he says, warm as a hearth, "surely there's an interest rate that you would find acceptable, given the increased risk of a lower margin distributor. We can come to some sort of arrangement there, right?"

The banker's shoulders drop a fraction. He has been offered a door, and the door has a number on it, and numbers are the country he lives in.

Brewbarry, however, is not finished being suspicious. "Yeah. I also find it a bit strange that she had this technique to get her prices down for supplying things, and then suddenly she tells me that she's got some supply chain issue that I should talk to you about. You tell me you don't know about it, and then you point me to this other guy who has found a way to magically get his costs down."

"No, no, no, no, no, no, no, no." Aurelan's hands are up. "Mr. Brewbarry, and Mr. Vukradin, I think your concerns are legitimate, but I — I would be remiss, I would be remiss as — look, if what you wish to do is to borrow—"

"How remiss you're gonna be if you continue down this path, my friend."

"You see, so — hold on, Mr. Brewbarry. I felt, as your banker, it was my duty to offer you a better path. But if you wish to do business with the Lionshield Coster—"

"Are you my wealth management consultant?"

Silence.

"Are you? No. You're the money man. So what you're gonna do is give us money so that we can do business."

Aurelan looks at the halberd, and at the aasimar smiling beside it, and at the drow who has corrected his arithmetic, and reaches the conclusion the whole room has already reached.

"Very well, Mr— well. Mr. Brewbarry."

"That's what I want to hear." The halberd goes back over his shoulder. "You want to be my friend, Aurelan."

I take in the faces at the edge of the room. Clerks, a few clients, one man in a great deal of silk. None of them look frightened. They look entertained, which in a counting house is a rarer and more dangerous emotion, and one the banker will have to manage after we have gone.

"But we'll be able to kickstart your business with—" Aurelan corrects himself, precise even now. "Linene will be able to kickstart your bathrobe business."

Brewbarry folds the paper away without reading it. "So now, let's go back to the topic of supply chain issues. Are you sure you don't know anything about it? Because she told me you would."

"Unfortunately, all I know is that there was some kind of system that Lionshield Coster was using that stopped working some time ago, for reasons that are obscure and apologetic. You'd have to consult House Margaster if you wanted to learn more. I personally know only that as a result of this sudden change in their shipping methods, the prices of goods shipped by Lionshield Coster have gone up considerably, and their business—"

A system that stopped. A rival house whose prices fell at the same moment. A banker who knows only the effect and refers everyone to the cause. He may be telling the truth about the limits of his knowledge; men in his position often are, because ignorance is cheaper to maintain than discretion. But the shape of it is the shape of a house that has found a way to profit from a machine failing and has no interest in the machine being repaired.

"This is how you do business, you know?" Brewbarry says. "You send people left and right? No, no. So you're gonna go to House Margaster, you're gonna find out what's going on, and I'm gonna come back tomorrow, and you're gonna tell me how I can fix it. You get it, Aurelan."

He has assigned the banker an errand. I have not seen anyone do that in a bank before.

"Anyway, it doesn't hurt to get multiple angles, so you're gonna go to this house, and you're gonna find out—"

"Yes, you are. I was about to say yes. I was about to say yes."

"You're gonna come back, and you're gonna give me what I need."

"Absolutely, mister."

"Good job."

Aurelan gathers what dignity remains and puts it to use. "Well, Mr. Brewbarry, I intend to find out what I can by tomorrow. I — you can rely on the Counting House."

"I'm a barbarian, I don't know much about business," Brewbarry allows, magnanimous now that he has won, "but when some wheel—" He is interrupted by his own thought, and finishes it anyway. "You turn, you oil it. That's what we're doing."
