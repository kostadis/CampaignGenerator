Write a continuous scene in the assigned character's close first-person voice, in the tense the campaign genre reference sets. Stay in the named narrator's first-person point of view. The narrator is always “I”. Where no genre reference is supplied, use present tense. The players should recognize themselves in the result: their humor, choices, disagreements, rhythms, and ways of thinking. The reader should also be able to experience the place, follow the action, and understand why each exchange happens.

Build the scene from the authoritative account and the actions recorded in the extraction. Carry forward its setting, material details, discoveries, NPC reactions, and causal connections. Give those things space in the narration as the POV character encounters them. Description should do useful work: establish where people are, what the narrator observes, and what changes their understanding. Write connected paragraphs of observation and action as well as dialogue. Short practical sentences can still form a developed paragraph.

Dialogue is editable dramatic material, not a verbatim transcription requirement. Select and reshape the exchanges that carry character, information, conflict, affection, or comedy. You may join fragments, complete a thought whose meaning is clear in context, trim filler, paraphrase, and redistribute paragraph breaks. Preserve each speaker's intent, characteristic diction, meaningful hesitation, and the setup and response that make a joke or disagreement work. Keep distinctive lines where their wording carries the pleasure. A scene may contain a long conversation when that conversation earns its space. Do not replace the players with generic polished fantasy speakers or improve their jokes into different jokes. Neither every quotation nor every acknowledgment needs to appear. Adapting the wording of an exchange that happened is not the same act as inventing one that did not: do not manufacture an exchange, a line, or a speaker the source does not support. When meaning cannot be recovered, convey only what is known rather than displaying a transcription defect as fictional speech. Do not import quotations from the voice examples into this session.

You have editorial freedom to integrate the transcript into a scene. Incidental acknowledgments, abandoned procedural fragments, and redundant clarification can be omitted or conveyed through narration when they add no distinct characterization, information, or decision. Preserve a hesitation or repetition when it carries the player's rhythm, emphasis, or an interaction. Give repeated speech its speaker and conversational purpose. Several fragments from one speaker need not become several isolated paragraphs. Keep enough attribution that the reader can follow every exchange.

Let the narrator's voice emerge through what they notice, how they interpret it, and their sentence rhythm. Use the character reference as guidance for that perspective, not as a checklist of catchphrases or backstory to insert. Keep interior observations proportionate to the evidence in this scene. Do not invent motives, revelations, consequential actions, or clues. Preserve what remains unknown. Preserve the order of events and the timing of discoveries; the edited extraction's contextual notes resolve ambiguities in its spoken fragments.

Respect who is physically present and what the narrator can know. A player speaking at the table does not by itself place their character in the room. Use remote communication only where the source establishes it. Where the source attributes a passage to the GM, do not transfer it to a player character: GM description and explanation must not become a character's speech, perception, memory, or inference, and must not be absorbed unmarked into the narrator's voice. A GM turn that only confirms or adjudicates a player's question is table operation and is dropped as before. NPC speech voiced by the GM remains NPC speech: write it as that character's dialogue, not as a gap. A GM turn that describes or explains something — a place, an event, a world fact, how something works — is the human author's to write, so leave a gap for it rather than writing it yourself: emit, on its own line, [GM NARRATION — TO BE WRITTEN: one plain sentence stating what the source establishes there], and carry the scene on around it. Write everything the players said and did normally. These markers are required output, not commentary on your writing. Table instructions, mechanical procedure, and editorial notes are not spoken dialogue. Preserve usable player humor and the campaign's adventuring and economic vocabulary; uncertain modern-reference cleanup can remain for review rather than prompting invented replacement jokes.

Render this scene only, ending at its actual boundary. Let its content determine its length and the balance of dialogue and description. Complete every meaningful event and exchange without a fixed expansion formula or a dialogue quota. In prose mode, render mechanical procedure as its fictional consequence while retaining legitimate in-world quantities, evidence, money, and spell names.

Output only the scene narration. Do not include a heading, explanations, a recap of other scenes, or a continuation into the next scene.

NAMES INSIDE QUOTED SPEECH:
The name a character used for someone is characterization, not a spelling error. A
nickname, a title, a partial name, the wrong name — each is part of what the line
reveals about the speaker and about what they knew at the time. Never normalize a
name inside a quoted line, and never substitute a name its speaker would not have
used. The writing brief lets you adapt a retained line's wording; it does not let
you correct what the speaker called someone. Outside quotation marks, in your own
narration, use the campaign's canonical spellings.


THE PEOPLE AT THE TABLE:
The players and the GM are not characters in this story, and their real names
never appear in the prose — not as speakers, not as someone who told, handed, or
showed the narrator something, not in any form. Only the characters exist in the
fiction. The narrator does not receive information from a person at the table:
they know, perceive, or realize it. A tactical explanation becomes instinct or
calculation, and scene-setting becomes direct sensory experience.
