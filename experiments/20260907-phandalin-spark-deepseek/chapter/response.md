# Chapter 51: We're famous. Why are we famous?

## Vukradin — Patrons of the Chord

The Common Chord is unrecognizable. Not the bones of it — same scarred beams, same worn floorboards, same corner where I used to play to three people and a very patient cat. But the air in here used to smell like spilled ale and honest desperation. Now it smells like expensive candles trying to impersonate something they're not.

I'm standing near the bar when I hear the complaint. A local, weathered fellow, clearly not one of Petra's new fashionable crowd, gesturing at the room with a tankard.

"Unaffordable now. All due to that Vukradin."

I feel Brewbarry tense beside me. I put a hand on his arm. The man hasn't noticed me. He's just a working man who misses when his local wasn't a spectacle.

I step forward. "Excuse me. I couldn't help but overhear."

He turns, ready to bristle, and then registers that he's talking to a seven-foot aasimar with silver eyes who is, in fact, the source of his complaint. But I'm not here to defend the Common Chord. I'm here to offer him something better.

"I'm actually planning on opening my own independent music studio," I say, letting the melody of the idea carry the words. "Low-cost. Nonprofit. A place where you could hear Lathander's Death on a regular basis without needing to take out a loan just to stand near the bar."

He blinks at me. The tankard lowers slightly.

"If you'd like to contribute to the charitable fund, that would speed things along considerably."

He's drunk. I can see that. But he's also listening, and that's the whole game, isn't it? Getting people to listen.

"You know what?" He fumbles at his belt and produces a small pouch. "I don't have a lot of money, man, but—" He presses ten gold pieces into my hand. "Here."

I stare at the coins. Ten gold. For a random nobody who'd rather choke on the Common Chord's new prices than pay them, that's not nothing. That's generosity.

"Wow. That's pretty generous, actually."

Soma, who's appeared at my elbow, nods her slow approval. "That's a hell of a donation."

I hold the gold up. "This goes in the studio fund. Not the regular coffers." I turn back to the man. "Thank you very much for contributing. That's awesome."

He's beaming now, drunk and pleased with himself. And then a thought crosses his face. "Do I get on—is there some kind of signage? For people who contributed?"

"Absolutely. Yes." I'm already organizing the structure in my head. "There are different tiers of giving levels. Ten gold pieces puts you at the—"

"Premier, or the Plus?" Soma offers. "Which one?"

"You're at the Plus level," I decide. "Not quite Premier yet." I look at the man. "Ten gold is a lot for a rando. You should be proud."

He is. He's very proud.

"We need his name," I tell Soma. "Write it down for the program notes."

"The—" She starts, gesturing vaguely.

"Per year. It's per year," I clarify. "If you want to be in next year's notes, you'll have to donate again. But a year is a long time."

Valphine's voice comes from somewhere behind me, dry as old parchment. "Yeah, we call this Patreon."

"It's my Patreon, exactly." I snap my fingers. "This is where it comes from. Patronage."

The evening stretches ahead of us before the 3 a.m. departure. We have time. And I have a cause.

"Before we go," I say, rolling my shoulders, "should I try to convince other people to contribute to the Studio Fund? How much time do we have?"

"Leaving at 3 AM," Soma says. "Plenty of time."

"Yeah." I survey the room. Five or six tables, patrons I don't recognize, all enjoying the Common Chord's new expensive atmosphere. "Let's hit up a few more people. Let's see how it goes here."

---

The second man recognizes me immediately — the real Vukradin, not just some rumor — but he shakes his head before I even finish my pitch. "Already done my charitable giving for this year."

"Okay, that's fine." I mean it. The whole point of clean funding is that you don't pressure people. "I'm not here to pressure anybody. The benefits are all on you. If you can be at the Plus or Premier level, I'm cool. I just like fans."

"Biggest fan," he says. "Biggest fan."

"Oh, bigger than Brewbarry?" I glance back at Brewbarry, who is, as always, enormous.

Valphine considers the man with professional interest. "Brewbarry's very large. In fact, he's huge in Japan sometimes."

"It's alright." I wave it off. "Nobody's as big as Brewbarry. That's just how it is."

Four more pitches, and each one lands like a chord resolving. The names come in — Wick, Sabbatha, Old Hesp, Ondrelle Kass — and I file them away for the program notes. These are the people who'll say they were there at the beginning.

Wick is the best. He has no idea who Lathander's Death is, doesn't even believe I'm the real Vukradin. But he's thrilled about the studio. "Putting together a music school," he says, nodding enthusiastically. "That's just—that's great, that's what the city needs."

"School, studio — they're all the same, right?" I explain to Soma. "If you have a studio, it's for training people. That's just how it goes."

Sabbatha, meanwhile, has been staring at Brewbarry with an expression I can only describe as appreciative assessment. "My goodness," she says, loud enough for the whole bar to hear. "You're very committed to the look, aren't you?"

"Yes," I confirm, because it's true. "He is quite bulging, isn't he?"

"Rippling," Soma adds.

"Exactly."

Brewbarry looks between us, uncertain whether he's being praised or studied. He settles on pleased. "Thank you," he says, and Sabbatha fans herself.

And then Old Hesp finds me. He's older than the Common Chord itself, I think, with the kind of face that's seen every era of this place and mourned each one.

"I remember when you used to play to an empty room," he says.

"Yeah?"

"Empty. Just me and the dust motes."

"Did you come?" I lean in. "Did I give you your money back at the end of—"

He shakes his head, almost laughing. "No, you didn't. You said that if I didn't give you a ten out of ten, you wouldn't—"

"I know, that's how honest I am." It's one of my better policies, honestly. Refunds for bad reviews. No musician with any self-respect should charge for a performance that doesn't move you. "Well, what do you think now?"

He looks around the Common Chord — the polished tables, the fashionable crowd, the candles trying to be classy. "Now this is a friggin' expensive place to be. Everybody's gotta look cool." He spits the last word like it's an insult.

"Well, come to the new studio, when it's up and running."

"It doesn't have the look," he says, and I know exactly what he means. "That feel. That gritty—"

"We're gonna get it back." I mean it. I can see it already, the low ceiling, the bare floors, the honest acoustics of a room built for music and not for profit. "We're gonna get it back."

"This is too commercial."

"Yeah." I look around the room that made me, that broke me, that spat me out unpaid and unknown. "Yeah, I agree."

"Petra's turned this into a money-making operation. I gotta respect her, she—she turned—I mean, I remember when that table—" He points to a spot near the stage, now covered in leather and crowned with a brass plaque. "The Brewbarry table. That was not leather and shiny and with a plaque."

"And we didn't have this kind of fancy food here," Soma adds, and Old Hesp nods vigorously.

"Right! Fancy food! We didn't have fancy food!"

I hold up my hands. "Alright. We'll fix it. We'll get the grit back, I promise."

The evening's tally sits in my head: twenty gold pieces for the studio fund, four new names for the program notes, one drunk man who now believes in the future of independent music in Neverwinter. Plus the separate five hundred Petra still owes me for the likeness rights, pending the performance in two days.

And also — I've learned something valuable from the ambient conversation of the bar. The sewer tides. Two highs, two lows each day, on a six-hour cycle from the river and the sea. If we time this right, we can be in and out before the water rises.

"We should get to Lim's Food Deliveries by 3 AM," I say as we gather ourselves. "That puts us at low tide, with about three, maybe four hours before things get uncomfortable."

Soma nods. "And then we can cast whatever spells we need in order to be ready."

"Exactly."

Valphine arches an eyebrow at me. "I'm curious. What's your take on House Margaster, then? Impressed with her control, or unimpressed?"

I consider this. House Margaster controls the shipping lanes that make Brewbarry's bathrobe business expensive. They own the logistics. They deliver on time, on schedule, and they know it. They're the ones who can make things happen.

"They're the competition." The words come out flatter than I intended.

"Okay. That I understand."

Lord Neverember. The name bubbles up again. "Right. And Lord Neverember—Lord Neverflaming—has a new drow advisor."

"Great," Soma says. "Isn't that Valphine?"

"No."

"Oh. Right."

"It's a rumor," I add. "But I suspect Valphine will know this advisor at dinner. We'll see."

Soma shrugs. "Everyone's related to everyone."

"Expected," she and Valphine say at the same time, and I decide not to ask.

We've secured the capital for Brewbarry's business. We have walkie-talkies in the form of paired Sending Stones. We have a plan, a time, and a tide. What could possibly go wrong?

## Vukradin — Nine Crates at Low Tide

The sewer grill sits at the bottom of aqueduct-like steps, wet stone slick beneath my boots. The air smells like river and rot and old secrets. It's just past 3 a.m., low tide, and the crates are already here.

Two carriers, moving with the unhurried rhythm of men who've done this a hundred times. They drop the crates near the grille — eight of them stacked together with the easy confidence of routine, and one, the ninth, placed separately on a ledge. The one who handles the ninth pauses. Looks down into the dark, as if checking that something is waiting below. Then he walks away.

The two men don't look at each other. They split in different directions, pretending to be strangers. It's almost insulting, how practiced the lie is.

"Right," I breathe. "Lim's gonna come out and take eight of the crates. She's not gonna take a ninth. I stick around, stealthily wait to see who comes for it, and then track it."

Soma nods. "I'd follow the guy who paid attention to the ledge. He knows something's coming out of that thing."

"Then it's settled." Valphine scans the departing figures. "Everyone other than Vukradin trails these two, and Vukradin trails the crate."

"Yes."

"Yes."

I position myself in the shadows of the aqueduct steps, my Boots of Elvenkind muffling every footfall. Soma's rat familiar skitters along the wall nearby, a tiny scout in the dark. We wait.

Minutes pass. The sounds of the city's night shift drift down from above—a cart, a distant argument, someone's dog.

And then something comes up for the ninth crate.

It's not a person. Small, quick, brass and leather, cat-sized, moving with the unhurried competence of a thing following instructions. It takes the crate and goes back down into the dark below.

"Okay," I murmur. "Vukradin will pursue stealthily."

Soma grabs my arm. "This is my concern. You end up going into the sewers now, and—"

"I'll be fine."

"One person in the sewers? Alright." She releases me with obvious reluctance. "Tell me the odds."

I do the math. Stealth bonus with my boots, advantage on moving silently. The chance of catastrophic failure is—

"Only a one in four hundred chance of disaster. It'll be fine."

"You know," the GM—no, the world—seems to say through the night air, "this is the end of Vukradin."

I ignore the omen. I descend.

---

The tunnel swallows me quickly. The construct moves ahead of me with mechanical purpose, a tiny silhouette of brass and leather that knows exactly where it's going. I follow at a careful distance, my footsteps silent on the wet stone.

Eight crates, no Commission Harbor mark. Smuggled in somehow, around the shipping lanes that House Margaster controls. And one crate, separated, claimed by a machine that answers to someone.

The tunnel branches. The construct takes the left passage, and I follow. Ahead, the faint glow of something—torchlight? Phosphorescence? The air grows warmer, thicker.

And then something shifts.

I stop mid-step. The tunnel ahead of me is suddenly... uninteresting. I stare at the passage where the construct disappeared, and I find that I don't care. It's boring. It's pointless. There's no reason to keep going this way.

I blink. That's wrong. I was following something. A crate. A construct. I came down here for a reason.

But the thought dissolves as quickly as it forms. What was I doing? Oh, right—something about a delivery. But this passage, this particular route, it's just not worth my time. I can feel it in my bones. Whatever's down that way, it's not important.

I turn and walk away. The decision feels weightless, natural, like I've simply decided the venture isn't profitable and moved on with sensible judgment.

It's not until I'm several hundred feet down a different tunnel that I stop and frown.

I remember where I was. I remember what I was doing. But the idea of continuing that path has simply stopped being interesting, as if someone reached into my head and quietly removed the hook that was pulling me forward. Not confusion. Just... boredom. A conviction, utterly reasonable, that the wrong way was the right way after all.

"Well," I say to the empty tunnel. "That's not good."

I look around. I have absolutely no idea where I am.

## Vukradin — Lost in Rsolk's Tunnels

The tunnel opens into a wider chamber, and I walk straight into a man.

He's standing in front of a group of others, and his expression cycles through confusion, suspicion, and back to confusion as he takes me in. He's rough-edged, practical, the kind of man who's been underground so long he reads the quality of torchlight the way other people read weather.

"How the hell did you get over here?"

I consider my options. I could bluff. I could brazen it out. But the honest answer is also the simplest.

"I don't know."

He stares at me.

"Where am I?"

He keeps staring. The men behind him shift, hands hovering near weapons.

"Because I'm not over there," I add, gesturing vaguely behind me. Which is true. I'm not where they think I should be. I'm here instead. That's the whole problem.

One of my rolls—the kind I'm not good at—tells me nothing about this man's motives. He's not angry, I can feel that much. He's confused. Genuinely puzzled about what I'm doing here.

"But I don't know what I'm doing here," I say, and it comes out more plaintive than I intended.

The man licks his finger, a strange gesture, and gestures at the ground beneath his feet. "Well. This is my territory."

I repeat it. "This is your territory."

"Yes. This is my territory."

"Okay." I take a step back. "Did you want me to go?"

"I'd want to know why you're in my territory."

"Oh, I'm sorry that I got lost." I find my best apologetic smile. "I'm happy to leave. Could you point me in the direction of Lim's?"

His expression sharpens. "Lim's Café?"

"Yeah, that's where I want to be headed."

"We're friends," Soma adds helpfully from behind me—no, wait, she's not here. That's my own voice. "Lim's a fan."

The man's eyes narrow. "Lim's Café?" he repeats, and suddenly he's very interested. The suspicion in his posture deepens. He's toying with something in his head, weighing whether I'm worth the trouble.

Then his face changes. A flicker of recognition.

"Are you that singer?"

"Exactly. And Lim's a fan."

"What are you doing down here, man?"

"I told you, I got lost!"

He studies me for a long moment. Then, slowly, wonder replaces suspicion.

"Vukradin... you're Vukradin, right?"

From behind him, one of his associates is beaming. "This is... is this really Vukradin?"

"Do you guys want autographs?"

They exchange looks. The answer is yes, obviously, but they recover quickly.

"We'd love some autographs."

"Sure, we can do some autographs, but then I gotta be going." I look at the leader. "Point me in the right direction."

He grins. "You know what, we're gonna do one better. We're gonna take you there."

"Alright. Sounds good."

"We don't want you to get lost again, Mr. Vukradin."

"Yeah, exactly." I'm already trying to figure out how to work this into a Sending update to Soma. She's going to lose her mind.

"Lim will be thrilled," I add.

The leader pauses. When he speaks again, his voice has lost its welcome warmth. "It's very dangerous down here."

"Is it?"

"Yeah, it's–it's–"

"It's dangerous in your territory?" I blink. "I would think you'd keep it very safe. You seem like a safe individual. I'm sure you keep the place shipshape."

"Mr. Vukradin, I agree with you."

"Fantastic."

"But I have to tell you why I was out here." He scratches his chin. "I was concerned when I saw you, and I'm very glad I recognized you. One of my guys tells me you have a deal with a friend of mine."

"At the Common Chord?"

"No, no. At the Toe Mine."

"Mountain's Toe Mine." The memory surfaces—dust, stone, the promise of ore that hasn't quite materialized. "Oh, yeah."

"Zeleen."

"Oh, I didn't know you knew him."

"Yeah, he said that he had a business relationship with you. I said he was full of shit."

"No, no, that's true, that's true." I almost laugh. "Yeah, they haven't produced very much yet, but they say they're working hard."

He laughs, a rough bark that echoes off the tunnel walls. "That's what he tells me, too!"

"That's how I feel about it, also."

He laughs again, and the tension in the chamber breaks. "Look, I manage the tunnels here."

"Oh, you're the tunnel manager."

"Yeah, a tunnel manager." He gestures around us. "Not all the tunnels, but the tunnels in this area."

"The tunnels in this area." I nod slowly. "Interesting. Okay."

His expression turns serious. "I've been having this problem with the tunnels. Two months ago, things started coming up the floodline."

"Floodline?"

He describes it—a tunnel that floods regularly, a stretch of the network that must have been miserable even before something moved in. "I've lost men," he says. "So we don't go there anymore."

I feel the familiar itch. It's the same itch that made me stop and help Elara with her overturned cart, the same one that dragged me into the Tower of Storms. "Do you need help with that? My friends and I have some abilities, and we're looking for ways to make money. Maybe we can help you."

He starts to answer, and I see it in his face—the relief of a man who's been carrying a problem alone. He really wants my help. He really wants my help.

"One of the guys who escaped said there was some kind of creature they'd never seen before."

"Oh. Creature stuff."

"It looked like a spider, but it had all the wrong colors."

"Okay."

"The webs it created were all wrong. Something really off about them."

I remember those webs. Strange webbing, unnatural colors, the wrongness of them in a place where everything else obeyed the natural order. "Yeah, we have experience with that."

He takes a breath. "Mr. Vukradin, I know this is too much to ask, but could you get me some tickets to the Common Chord?"

"I can ask." I hold up a hand. "We have kind of an arm's-length relationship with the Common Chord, but I will ask for you."

"That's all I could ask for."

"Alright, let's do some autographs, and then we gotta be on our way."

He's already pulling a scrap of paper from his pocket. "One more thing. What do you know about the spiders?"

I blink at him. "What do I know about—" I shake my head. "I don't know anything about tunnels, man, I'm a musician."

"No, the—the spiders. The ones in the tunnel. The floodline. We were talking about that."

"Oh! Right. Yeah." I look at the entrance he gestured toward earlier. "The ones with the wrong colors? The unnatural webs?"

"Yes. Those."

"We've dealt with them before. They're—" I pause, trying to find the words. "They're wrong. Not like normal spiders. The webs are strange, like they're trying to be something they're not."

He nods slowly. "That's what my man said."

"Whatever's coming up through that floodline," I say, "it's the same kind of thing we found before. We can handle it."

"Good." He claps me on the shoulder. "Good. We'll talk more when you come back."

Autographs exchanged, directions given, escort arranged. We move through the tunnels together, my new friends and I, and I'm already composing the Sending to Soma.

The spider job is fair-trade gold. Finally, a business opportunity.

## Soma — Denvar's Unsettled Account

He eats his breakfast at four in the morning like it's the most normal thing in the world.

We watch from across the street, tucked into a doorway that smells like old bread and cat piss. Denvar sits alone at the cook shop, working through a plate of something that steams in the cold air. He looks ordinary. Unremarkable. A man who carries things for a living.

"A dinner at 4 a.m.," I mutter.

"It's breakfast," Valphine corrects.

"Whatever."

He finishes, pays, and walks. We follow at a careful distance. The city is quiet at this hour—the Bluelake District is mostly shadows and shuttered windows, the kind of neighborhood where people don't ask questions because they can't afford the answers.

He stops at a building with many rooms and a light burning in the window. Steps inside. A few minutes later, a window on the second floor goes dark.

"He's asleep," Valphine observes.

"And we know where he lives." I watch the building. "Single-room occupancy. High-efficiency flat. Has to have a landlord."

"Who we could potentially ask questions about him of."

"Right." The sky is still dark. "But it's the middle of the night."

Valphine considers. "We can wait till morning. Seven, maybe."

The rat can scout now, though. I've already sent it in through a gap in the foundation, a tiny shadow moving through a building full of sleeping strangers.

Just a sight-seeing expedition. Look for odd signs, receipts, pay stubs—anything to get a sense of the man.

Through the rat's eyes, I see his room. Small, neat, utilitarian. A bed, a table, a chair. Clothes folded with the precision of a man who doesn't own much. And on the table, a stack of paper.

I guide the rat closer. Delivery chits. Seven to ten days of them, in order. Each one reads the same: "9 crates, 3rd hour, 8 up, 1 down." A client reference. A house mark. No name anywhere.

And every single one carries a settlement stamp except the last. The newest one, the one corresponding to the delivery we just watched at the sewer grate.

"This guy is what he is," I murmur, pulling back from the rat's senses. "He's a guy who collects things."

"But he's making more money than you'd expect," Valphine says.

"Yeah. That's what I'm saying." I gesture at the cook shop, still visible down the street. "Somebody who just carries goods doesn't spend money at a restaurant at 4 AM. He's a little overcompensated for his job."

"Any identifying marks? Receipts?"

"No. Nothing. No insignia." I almost laugh. "He's just a guy who apparently found a good gig."

"Oh shit, I want that gig," I say without thinking.

"If you want to run drugs, that's available as a career, Soma," Vukradin's voice crackles through the Sending Stone, "but I don't recommend it."

"Aww."

The building has a door that opens into a small office area, and a lamp burns there. A man sits inside—the landlord, presumably, keeping whatever hours landlords keep in a place like this. He's got the look of someone who's seen everything and is impressed by none of it.

We exchange glances. We could wait till morning. But the door is right there, and I'm done being patient.

"Alright," I say. "We just had some questions. We wanted to know—what's it cost to live here? I think that's the first one we need to know."

The landlord looks at me. Up and down, the way people do when they're trying to figure out what shelf you belong on.

"I don't see many turtles around here."

"That's not what I asked."

He shrugs, unbothered. "I thought turtles carried their house on their shoulders, so they didn't need a place to stay."

I feel my jaw tighten. This is what I get for leaving my cabin. "I see what you think of us. I can't live in this thing." I tap my shell. "It merely protects me. I actually have a nice cabin out in the woods, out by—" I wave a hand. "Outside Neverwinter."

"Oh, well, I learned a new thing." He seems genuinely surprised. "I didn't know turtles didn't live in their shells. I guess you're not like turtles."

"No, no, we have—very—" I take a breath. "That's kind of slanderous to us."

He notices Valphine then. And Brewbarry, looming in the doorway with his halberd. Realization crosses his face like a cold wind.

"I'm just asking a question."

"Oh, I'm just asking questions, I see." I smile at him. "Just asking questions guy has entered the chat."

"Just asking questions."

"Look." I lean on the counter. "I'll pretend I didn't hear some of that. I just want to know what it costs to live here, and what kind of people normally stay in a place like this. You could say I'm looking for a place for my—my shell sprouts in the future."

"Look, first of all, we don't care." He holds up both hands. "We're an equal-opportunity housing establishment."

"It's so progressive."

"It isn't about being progressive." He shrugs again. "Money's money, man. I don't care who's got it." He turns to Valphine. "I ask every question. You're a drow."

"Very astute."

"I thought drow didn't like the sunlight."

"Oh, I love the sunlight."

"I love the sunlight," she adds, for emphasis.

"Every conversation about race," Vukradin mutters through the Sending Stone, "but he's not racist."

"He's asking questions!" the landlord protests.

"It isn't our duty to educate him," I say. "He needs to learn on his own. He needs to put in the work."

Valphine asks something about "KYC laws" and I don't fully track it, but the landlord doesn't either, which seems to be the point.

"So you just take money and don't ask questions." I tap the counter. "You don't know anything about your tenants?"

"I don't scry on my customers."

"I don't believe that." I hold his gaze. "Given the racism you presume, I'm sure you scry on certain ones of them, don't you?"

He glances at Brewbarry again. There's a long pause.

"Look, I don't want any trouble. If you want a place to stay here, I'll give you a discount."

"Really, we just want to know about one of your tenants."

"...Alright."

The name comes easily once we ask. "You mean Denvar?"

Denvar. Just like that, we've got more than we came with. We've got a name.

"He dropped something last night," I explain. "We saw him skulking about in the alleyway super late at night, and he dropped a little pouch. We'd like to return it to him."

"Making a delivery," Valphine adds.

The landlord considers us. Then he shrugs. "He's got chits recording the job."

"Yeah, see, this is what the rat was going in looking for."

"He has chits recording the job," the landlord repeats. "Seven to ten days of them, in order. Each one reads '9 crates, 3rd hour, 8 up, 1 down.' There's a weekly settlement. Client reference, house mark, no name anywhere."

"And all his deliveries... one hundred percent of his delivery chits are the same? He literally has one job."

"Yeah. It is. Wow." I shake my head. "Yeah, no, that's not suspicious."

"They all look settled," the landlord says. "They have—"

"The checks on them, whatever. And stamp of payment." I nod. "But nothing identifiable other than—"

"They clearly seem to be related to this one contract."

"Yes."

"And today's later delivery is the one chit that hasn't been settled."

"So there's seven, but the seventh one has not been paid for."

"And that was the one he just, presumably, delivered." I straighten up. "So I guess we have reason to follow him once he wakes up, don't we?"

The landlord is watching me. "He owes me some money today, you know. Good customer, quiet, doesn't bother anybody. But he's got until the end of the day."

"So he's motivated." I can see the shape of it now. A man with one job, one source of income, an urgent deadline. He'll go collect his payment as soon as he wakes. "If he doesn't pay up today, he won't be here tomorrow."

"He's got until the end of the day, and he's a good customer. He's quiet. He doesn't bother anybody."

"I'll bet."

"Good guy, huh?" Valphine says.

"Yeah, totally good guy."

"You don't think he's a serial killer or anything?" I ask.

The landlord looks at us—at me with Meril's staff, at Valphine in her white armor with the glowing eyes, at Brewbarry with his halberd. His expression shifts.

"Should I be worried?"

"You never know with these types. You never know."

I let it hang there.

"I'd be worried about him," Valphine adds, pleasantly.

The landlord is quiet for a moment.

"So, Soma, right? You introduced yourself at some point."

"Yes, I definitely did."

"Soma, now that I know turtles are looking for housing, perhaps you could tell me—what makes a good turtle room?"

I consider my options. I could mislead him. Give him some nonsense about sand pits and humidity. But that would be a lie, and the truth costs me nothing.

"A nice hearth," I say. "Plenty of water. Being near a body of water is nice."

"All turtles are the same, right?" Vukradin crackles.

"Yes, we—I speak for all turtles. I'm the Jesse Jackson of turtles."

The landlord looks genuinely thoughtful. "A hearth. Plenty of water."

"That's what I said."

He nods, filing it away. "Alright. Well—if you're looking for a room—"

"We'll be back." I step back from the counter. "We've got what we came for."

Denvar. One job. One unsettled chit. One deadline he can't afford to miss. And when he wakes, he'll go to collect his money.

We'll be waiting.

## Soma — Harpers Behind the Wall

A few hours later, Denvar wakes.

I've recast Find Familiar in the interim, spent the slot to restore my rat from its scouting duties. It's resting in my sleeve now, a tiny weight against my wrist, as we watch Denvar emerge from the building. He looks well-rested. Content. A man who had a good breakfast and is ready to face the day.

He walks through the Bluelake District with the easy confidence of someone who knows exactly where he's going. We follow at a distance. Vukradin has rejoined us after his adventure in the sewers, and the Sending Stone updates have given us the shape of what happened—new friends, a tunnel manager named Rsolk, a spider problem that might become a paying job.

"We just gotta clear out these spiders," Vukradin says, practically bouncing. "And it's more money for the studio. This is great. Finally a business opportunity. Fair Trade Gold."

"Sure," I say. "Fair trade."

Denvar stops at a stone wall adjoining a building. He looks around, trying to be discreet—the kind of look that draws more attention than it deflects. Then he pulls open a stone, reaches inside, and retrieves a bag. He looks at the chit, smiles, and closes the hiding place.

"Payday!" I say. "I'm going to the casino!"

Then Denvar is gone, walking away with the satisfied stride of a man whose debts are settled.

"It's a dead drop," Vukradin says.

"It's a stone that backs into a building," I observe.

"Is it like a two-way doctor's-office window? Where you put it in on one side and close the door, and then they—"

"We'd have to go inspect it," Valphine says.

"Yeah. Effectively a dead drop. So we should let him go and inspect it."

"Excellent."

I watch Denvar's retreating figure. He's already a footnote in this investigation, a piece we've read and set aside. "Barring coming back the next evening before he leaves his house—but we don't know exactly how early he has to leave to make these drops." I shake my head. "Denvar might be a dead end."

"Yeah."

That's fine. The wall is more interesting now. We approach it carefully, staying close to the building's side. The stone that Denvar moved is set flush, indistinguishable from its neighbors unless you know what to look for. Inside, a compartment just large enough for a pouch or a stack of chits.

"It's a two-way rock," Vukradin observes. "We can't physically open the other side, but—" He brightens. "I have an idea. The Knock spell. We could open our side, cast Knock on the other side, and then send the rat through to look around."

"If you propose it, I think it's worth a shot."

"And then we could look in and see what room we're looking at, try to find the right room, or we could even send one of your rats in there to look around."

"I like that combo."

Valphine raises a hand. "I have a different strategy, which is, we go in there and offer them a business proposal."

"Yeah, Vukradin could play."

"Your booking agency."

"Getting backstage would give you access to staff-only areas where you could do your music preparation—and by music preparation—"

"There you go."

"We could cast two spells," Vukradin says, "or we could have an extended contract process, and several performance roles, and hours of time." He pauses. "I do like to perform, though."

"Yeah, see, I mean... these are tough choices."

"Let's do both! Porque no los dos."

"Exactly. Why not?"

The decision is made quickly. We approach the wall as a unit, settle into positions that screen us from the street, and set to work.

Vukradin's spell opens our side of the dead drop. The inner closure resists—a latch or bar on the far side, meant to be operated from within the building. He gestures, speaks the word, and the lock clicks open. A second closure, a panel of stone, swings inward.

I send the rat through.

The tunnel of the dead drop opens into a room. No windows. A back office, clearly, with a desk and papers and supplies. There's a kind of organized chaos to it—the working space of someone who handles correspondence and records, someone who expects to be undisturbed.

The rat creeps along the edge of the room, keeping to the shadows. The desk is covered with ledgers, loose sheets, the kind of administrative mess that suggests real activity. And pinned to the wall, in a place where anyone entering would see it—

"Oh." The word escapes me.

The symbol is unmistakable. A musical note, wrapped in a harp's curve, rendered in oiled bronze and mounted like a dedication plaque.

"Harp?" Vukradin says.

"Oh, wow!"

"It's a Harper symbol," I say slowly. The rat is sitting on the desk now, looking directly at it. "This is a Harper... headquarters. The local Harpers."

The room is what it is. A back office with no windows, a desk with papers and supplies, and the symbol of the Harpers mounted on the wall like a claim of ownership.

"So that's... weird." I pull back from the rat's senses. "Harper. So that's—the Harpers are smuggling?"

"Well, the Harpers aren't smuggling, or they're arranging for that ninth crate?"

"Clearly that ninth crate involves some smuggling because it's being directly delivered." I look at the others. "So yes, the Harpers are smuggling in some capacity."

"There's no—the only good people are the Order of the Gauntlet."

"Debatable," I say.

"Are there?" Valphine asks.

"You know, they are."

"Reformed," Vukradin says. "They're reformed."

"Why?"

"Well, they had that whole thing with the barbarians, you know."

"Well, that was—they were misled by their political masters."

"Exactly, that's what I'm saying. They've been past that. They're reformed now."

"Right, and so they've now rejected those political masters."

"Basically," Vukradin concludes, "they're the Reformed Order of the Gauntlet."

"Yes. They recognized that they were used by powerful forces, and now they're all about the little guy."

"Truth, justice, and the American Way."

"Different comic book," Vukradin says. "Alright."

The rat is still in the Harper office, a tiny spy among the ledgers. We know what this place is now. The ninth crate trail leads here—to a windowless basement office beneath a tavern.

"The House of a Thousand Faces," Valphine reads from the sign around front.

Brewbarry, who's been uncharacteristically quiet, clears his throat. "I've heard of this place. When I first came to Neverwinter, I couldn't go there. I didn't have the money."

"He was more of a Common Chord slum," Vukradin explains. "And this was a high-end establishment."

"Yeah." Brewbarry looks at the door—solid oak, brass fittings, the kind of door that says you should have made a reservation. "I think I'd heard about this place."

"He's a man of class now," I say. "He can buy his way into high society."

"Isn't it obvious?" Valphine says. "It's the quality of your talent."

"Not."

"It's totally not. You sure."

"It definitely is not." Vukradin shakes his head. "There is a force behind our fame. Some groups are maneuvering—we're being—"

"Manipulated," I finish.

"To their advantage for some reason."

It's true. The fame has never made sense. A band that played to empty rooms a year ago, suddenly selling out the hardest ticket in Neverwinter? Someone built this. Someone's using us.

But that's a problem for later. Right now, we have a dead drop, a Harper office, and a tavern with a name that suggests a thousand different faces.

The fire inside draws properly, Vukradin says when he peers through the window. Mirrors on the walls, mannequins wearing antiquated fashions, lush couches. A high-end place in the low-end Bluelake District. Something off about it.

I recall the rat. It's spent. A tiny body, a brief life, a job well done.

"Alright," I say, turning to face the House of a Thousand Faces. "Let's go see what the Harpers are hiding."

Brewbarry is already at the door.
