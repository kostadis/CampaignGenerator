## Valphine — Bullying Through the Loan

The banker is called Aurelan, and he has already made the mistake that surface functionaries make in every counting room I have ever entered: he has assumed that the man in front of him is the client, when the man in front of him is the weapon.

"You're not gonna help me," Brewbarry tells him, leaning over the desk so that its lamp lights him from beneath. "I'm gonna help you, old man. Don't get things confused in that little head of yours." He straightens. "Now, please tell me, is there some business to be done here, or should I go elsewhere?"

Aurelan smooths the letter from Linene flat with both hands, which is what a man does when he wants to look at anything other than the barbarian. "Well," he says. "What are you making?"

"What do you mean, what am I baking?"

"Making."

"Am I supposed to tell you what I'm making? I've got this letter here."

"Yes, well." Aurelan adopts the small patient voice of a clerk who has decided he is dealing with a child. "What the letter says is that Linene wishes us to lend her money so that she can start her business. She does not describe the nature of the business."

Brewbarry considers this the way he considers a door he has been asked to open rather than break. "I'm gonna show the world how cruel this world is," he says at last, "and I'm giving this softness to all the good people of the Forgotten Realms by supplying them with bathrobes."

I file the sentence away. It is, structurally, the founding creed of a religion, and he has produced it without noticing. The Temple of Lathander's Searing Pain of Justice was assembled with more deliberation and less conviction.

"That's very interesting," says Aurelan, and reaches for a scrap of paper and a quill, and begins to write. "Do you know how much this will cost to make?"

"I have absolutely no idea. Which is why I have a wealth management consultant. I suggest that you talk to her."

"Very well. I will make a note of talking to her." The quill scratches. "And how do you intend to ship your goods?"

"What do I look like? Some merchant? The logistics guy?" Brewbarry spreads his hands to display himself, halberd, hides, and all. "No. I hack and slash."

At my shoulder Soma has folded her arms across her shell and is watching Aurelan's quill rather than his face, which is the correct thing to watch. Vukradin has his head tilted, listening to the exchange as if to a piece in an unfamiliar key, waiting to learn where the resolution lies before he adds his voice. He will add it. He always does.

Aurelan's pen stops. "Oh," he says, in the voice of a man who has read the second page. "I see you intend to use Lionshield Coster as your shipping agent and business arrangement." A pause of exactly the length required to arrange a regret. "Mr. Brewbarry, I wish to apologize. I want you to know that I have no objection to Lionshield Coster's business, and I have done wonderful business with them, but their rates have gone ridiculously up, and I am afraid that based on my calculations—"

[GM NARRATION — TO BE WRITTEN: Aurelan produces a sheet of calculations the party cannot read.]

"He counts fast, for an old man," Brewbarry says, not quietly.

"He counts," Soma says. "It's what he's for."

"—with the margins we expect," Aurelan continues over them, "even with Margaster's very generous margins and prices, and the cost of moving goods in and out of Phandalin, plus—" He is warming to it now, the arithmetic a wall he can put between himself and the halberd. "I don't think—I feel that the Counting House—"

"Sorry, let me—let me cut you off." Brewbarry raises a hand. "Yadda yadda yadda yadda. You get my blood pumping. Just tell me you're gonna get it done, and that's it. Otherwise I'm gonna get upset." He lets the hand drop. "You don't want to see me upset."

I have watched Brewbarry upset. It is a clean thing, without malice, like a landslide. Aurelan has not watched it and is therefore in the unusual position of being more frightened than he needs to be by the threat and less frightened than he should be by the man.

But the banker's sums annoy me, and not because they are illegible. They are incomplete, and a tactician cannot let a wrong count stand in a room where it will be repeated.

"We're not tallying in two additional factors," I say.

Aurelan's head comes up. He had not, I think, registered me as a party to the negotiation, only as a shadow at the barbarian's flank, which is where I prefer to stand.

"The temple markup," I say, "and the church of Lathander's bulk demand."

He looks at me properly, then. I can watch his eye travel: the holy symbol first, because it is what he has been trained to price. "Oh," he says. "You are one of the blessed of Lathander."

A pause, during which his eye completes the rest of the journey, skin and hair and the shape of the ears.

"And you're a drow."

"You're very perceptive, Aurelan," Brewbarry says, dry as a ledger.

I do not correct the man. Surface dwellers are so easily surprised by the obvious that correcting them only extends the surprise. Lathander's light falls on every dawn without asking who stands beneath it; that a daughter of House Sotorra chose to stand there is a calculation, not a miracle, and I have never understood why the calculation should astonish anyone but my sisters.

Aurelan recovers by retreating into procedure, which is where such men keep their courage. "But Mr. Brewbarry, I wish to inform you that I do have a solution to your problem." He folds his hands. "Since your business is in production, what I would suggest is that instead of using Lionshield Coster for distribution of goods, you reach out to House Margaster. They are the ones who have managed to reduce the pricing of goods quite significantly, and I believe with their margins and their arrangements you would be able to run the business most efficiently. And if you could secure a letter of agreement with them—a letter of agreement—we would be more than delighted to sign on the dotted line."

There it is. In Menzoberranzan a broker who steers you toward a rival house at the moment you need him most is either owned by that house or hoping to be, and one does not need to know which to know what he is. I feel the small, pleasant tightening behind my sternum that comes of a pattern completing itself. I glance at Brewbarry to see whether he has followed it.

He has. Not the way I have, along the lines of leverage and obligation, but by the plainer route of having been lied to before and recognizing the smell.

"Do I look like a fool?" he asks.

Aurelan opens his mouth.

"Do I look like a fool, Aurelan?"

The halberd comes off his back in a single practiced motion and settles across his palm, the haft tapping his other hand in a slow, even rhythm—not a threat, exactly, but the sound a threat makes while it waits to be needed. I note, with some appreciation, that the tempo does not vary.

"Exactly," Brewbarry says, as though Aurelan had answered. "And you tell me to go and do some business with somebody else. You think I'm an idiot? Did you have, like, some sweet deal with this other guy? I didn't give two shit about it." Tap. "Now, I told you. I have a wealth management consultant. She has a supply company. She's gonna borrow money from you. You're gonna lend it to her. And you're gonna get this done."

Vukradin chooses his entrance here, as I knew he would—after the blade has set the key and before the banker has had time to decide he is brave. He steps up beside Brewbarry with his palms open, the picture of a reasonable man rescuing a difficult one.

"Surely," he says, "surely there's an interest rate that you would find acceptable, given the increased risk of, you know, a lower-margin distributor. We can come to some sort of arrangement there, right?"

It is beautifully done. The halberd has made the threat; the bard offers the banker a door with a smaller number painted on it, so that when he walks through it he can tell himself he negotiated. I have seen matron mothers' envoys do this with less grace and more poison. Vukradin will insist afterward that he meant every word of it, and I have stopped arguing the point with him; a maneuver that good does not require its author to know it is a maneuver.

"Yeah," Brewbarry says, unwilling to let the pressure ease. "I also find it a bit strange that she had this technique to get her prices down for supplying things, and then suddenly she tells me she's got some supply chain issue that I should talk to you about. You tell me you don't know about it, and then you point me to this other guy who has found a way to magically get his costs down."

"No, no, no, no, no, no, no, no." Aurelan has run out of ways to say the word before he runs out of need for it. "Mr. Brewbarry, and Mr. Vukradin, I think your concerns are legitimate, but I—I would be remiss, I would be remiss as—look, if what you wish to do is to borrow—"

"How remiss you're gonna be," Brewbarry says, "if you continue down this path, my friend."

"You see, so—hold on, Mr. Brewbarry. I felt, as your banker, it was my duty to offer you a better path. But if you wish to do business with the Lionshield Coster—"

"Are you my wealth management consultant?"

Aurelan blinks.

"Are you? No. You're the money man. So what you're gonna do is give us money so that we can do business."

The silence holds for a count of three, which in my experience is the length of time it takes a functionary to weigh his commission against his skin.

"Very well, Mr.—" Aurelan swallows the rest. "Well, Mr. Brewbarry."

"That's what I want to hear." The halberd goes back over his shoulder. "You want to be my friend, Aurelan."

[GM NARRATION — TO BE WRITTEN: A small crowd has gathered around the desk to watch, and Aurelan signs the loan paperwork, which is for a significant sum, and hands it over.]

"But we'll be able to kickstart your business with—" Aurelan corrects himself with the reflex of a man who has just remembered whose name is on the page. "Linene will be able to kickstart your bathrobe business."

The papers pass into Brewbarry's hands. He holds them as he holds anything that is not a weapon: carefully, as if they might turn out to be one.

Brewbarry does not leave the desk, which I had not expected and Aurelan visibly had. He folds the loan into his belt and sets both fists on the wood.

"So now," he says, "let's go back to the topic of supply chain issues. Are you sure you don't know anything about it? Because she told me you would."

Aurelan's shoulders have found some new, lower position that they will likely keep for the rest of the day. "Unfortunately, all I know is that there was some kind of system that Lionshield Coster was using that stopped working some time ago, for reasons that are obscure and—" he searches for the word and does not quite find it "—apologetic. You would have to go consult House Margaster if you wanted to learn more. I personally know only that as a result of this sudden change in their shipping methods, the prices of goods shipped by Lionshield Coster have gone up considerably, and their business—"

A system. He does not say what kind, and I do not think he is withholding; he has the face of a man repeating something he was told at a dinner. What is interesting is the shape of the thing rather than its name: the Coster's prices climbed when something stopped, and the house that profits from the climb is the house he was just sent to recommend. I do not need to know the mechanism to know the direction.

"This is how you do business, you know?" Brewbarry says. "You send people left and right? No. No. So you're gonna go to House Margaster. You're gonna find out what's going on. And I'm gonna come back tomorrow, and you're gonna tell me how I can fix it." He taps the desk once. "You get it, Aurelan. Money that I'm gonna give you."

House Margaster. The name lands in me with a familiar weight; it is the name that has been standing behind other names since we came to this town, the way a great house stands behind its merchant fronts. Every city has one. Menzoberranzan's version at least had the decency to be beautiful about it. This one, from everything we have seen, is merely large.

"Anyway," Brewbarry says, "it doesn't hurt to get multiple angles. So you're gonna go to this house, and you're gonna find out—"

"Yes. You are—I was about to say yes. I was about to say yes."

"You're gonna come back, and you're gonna give me what I need."

"Absolutely, mister."

"Good job."

Vukradin, behind me, makes a small satisfied sound that I would call a hum if I were the sort to describe him charitably. He is watching Brewbarry the way a conductor watches a soloist who has found the phrase on his own. I have no doubt he will tell him so, by name, before we reach the door.

Aurelan draws a breath and manages, with what dignity a man can gather while leaning away from a barbarian, the closest thing to a formal undertaking the morning has produced. "Well, Mr. Brewbarry. I intend to find out what I can by tomorrow. You can rely on the Counting House."

"I'm a barbarian," Brewbarry tells him, almost kindly. "I don't know much about business. But when some wheel—" He is interrupted by the crowd shifting, and finishes over it. "You turn, you oil it. That's what we're doing."

[GM NARRATION — TO BE WRITTEN: A wealthy client of the Counting House pushes forward from the crowd, dressed in silk imitations of Uthgardt barbarian hides; the effect is worse than cheap costume, but he is entirely sincere.]

I have seen this creature before, though never in silk. Every court breeds him: the courtier who mistakes the outward form of a dangerous man for the danger itself and thinks that by wearing the shape he has acquired the substance. In my sisters' halls he would have been used until he was amusing and then discarded. Here he is merely allowed to approach.

"What a genuine costume!" he says, and reaches for Brewbarry's sleeve.

"Are you touching my clothes?" Brewbarry does not turn his head. "Be careful."

"Wow. You are really into the bit. I am very impressed."

[GM NARRATION — TO BE WRITTEN: The man attempts to imitate Brewbarry's stance and bearing, and the result is awkward for everyone watching.]

Brewbarry's response is to give him nothing at all, not even the courtesy of contempt—the crowd, the desk, the loan in his belt, all of it apparently more real to him than a man in silk making a shape at his elbow. It is, I decide, the most exact humiliation available, and he has arrived at it without calculation. I am beginning to think that is his method for everything.

Vukradin, meanwhile, has remembered the one thread of the morning he has not yet resolved, and cannot leave it hanging. "I didn't find out what they count," he says, to Aurelan and to the room.

"We—we—we—" Aurelan looks at him with the exhaustion of a man being asked, at the end of everything, a question a child would have asked at the beginning. "The Counting House is a bank."

"Oh." Vukradin nods. "You could have just said you're a bank. All right."

"Yes. We count money."

I had assumed notes, or the beats in a measure, given the company I keep. Money is the more honest answer. It is the only thing in this room that everyone present has been counting since we walked in—Aurelan his commission, the silk man his standing, the crowd its entertainment, Vukradin his studio, Brewbarry the softness he intends to sell to a cruel world. Only Soma has been counting something else, and when I glance at her she is watching the door, measuring how long it will take to get her bale out through the press of bodies. She is the only one of us, I think, who has not once forgotten that we came here to leave.
